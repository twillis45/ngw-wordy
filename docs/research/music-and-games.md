## VERIFIED

### The label/scene geography (`rnb90s`)

The pack was rewritten to give each board a point of view per label and scene.
The four worlds are real and distinct, and the pack draws the lines correctly:

**Uptown Records — Andre Harrell, New York, 1986–1999.** Founded by Harrell in
1986 with an MCA distribution deal; signed Heavy D & the Boyz, Al B. Sure!,
Jodeci and Mary J. Blige, and employed Sean Combs as an A&R executive until
firing him in 1993. Harrell's stated bet was on the emerging new jack swing
sound, and the label is generally credited with the "hip-hop soul" fusion.

- BlackPast, *Uptown Records (1986–1999)* — https://blackpast.org/african-american-history/uptown-records-1986-1999/ (scholarship)
- uDiscover Music, *Andre Harrell: Remembering The Pioneer Of Hip-Hop Soul* — https://www.udiscovermusic.com/stories/andre-harrell-rip/ (journalism)
- Nelson George, *Reflecting on Uptown Records* — https://nelsongeorge.substack.com/p/reflecting-on-uptown-records (community/practitioner; George was there)

**Bad Boy — Sean Combs, New York, 1993.** Combs founded Bad Boy in 1993 after
being fired from Uptown; Clive Davis at Arista bankrolled it (~$1.5m) with
Arista distributing. First signee Craig Mack, then The Notorious B.I.G., Faith
Evans, Total, 112, Mase. The in-house production team was **the Hitmen** —
sample-driven, pop-loop-forward, built for crossover. That is a genuinely
different house sound from Uptown's, and the pack is right to separate them.

- Bad Boy Worldwide Entertainment Group company history, FundingUniverse — https://www.fundinguniverse.com/company-histories/bad-boy-worldwide-entertainment-group-history/ (journalism/business reference)

**LaFace — L.A. Reid and Babyface, Atlanta, 1989.** A joint venture with Arista.
TLC signed 1991; also Toni Braxton, Usher, OutKast, Xscape. Thirty-three number
one singles. Widely credited with building the infrastructure of the modern
Atlanta music industry.

- New Georgia Encyclopedia, *LaFace Records* — https://www.georgiaencyclopedia.org/articles/arts-culture/laface-records/ (scholarship)

### New jack swing and Teddy Riley (`rnb90s`, background)

The term was coined by Barry Michael Cooper in an 18 October 1987 *Village Voice*
profile of Teddy Riley. Riley produced Keith Sweat's *Make It Last Forever*
(1987, "I Want Her") and formed Guy the same year. The genre's origin point is
conventionally placed at 1987 — i.e. it is a **late-80s** invention that
*dominated* the early 90s. Any clue that dates its invention to the 90s would be
wrong; none currently does.

- Barry Michael Cooper, *Teddy Riley's New Jack Swing* — https://barrymichaelc.medium.com/teddy-rileys-new-jack-swing-harlem-gangsters-raise-a-genius-1f441da4e7f1 (primary — the man who named it)
- Carnegie Hall, *Timeline of African American Music: New Jack Swing* — https://timeline.carnegiehall.org/genres/new-jack-swing (scholarship)
- Red Bull Music Academy, *Teddy Riley and New Jack Swing: An Oral History* — https://daily.redbullmusicacademy.com/2017/05/teddy-riley-new-jack-swing-oral-history/ (practitioner oral history)

### Quiet storm as a radio format (`rnb90s`)

Melvin Lindsey, then a Howard University junior, went on air as a substitute DJ
at **WHUR-FM** in **May 1976**; general manager **Cathy Hughes** named the show
*The Quiet Storm* after the Smokey Robinson record — and after Lindsey himself
("Cathy said I was very quiet and I struck her like a storm"). By late 1977 it
was the most-listened show in DC, and the format was replicated at Black adult
stations nationally. It is a **format**, not a genre: a nightly slot, defined by
tempo, mood and the request line.

- WHUR 96.3 FM, *The Quiet Storm Station — History* — https://thequietstormstation.whur.com/history/ (primary — the station's own account)
- Essence, *Play Another Slow Jam: An Oral History of The Quiet Storm* — https://www.essence.com/entertainment/play-another-slow-jam-cathy-hughes-remembers-creating-the-quiet-storm/ (Hughes in her own words)
- Radio Hall of Fame, *Melvin Lindsey* — https://www.radiohalloffame.com/melvin-lindsey (institutional)

Three clues rest on this and all hold:

- `rnb90s / chorus / hours`: "Ten to two. The format, and the reason the phone lines opened." — **VERIFIED.** Correctly calls it a format; the ten-to-two slot and the request line are exactly how it worked.
- `rnb90s / soften / often`: "Twice an hour after ten at night, until the request line finally stopped ringing." — **VERIFIED** as consistent with the format.
- `rnb90s / chorus / such`: "The word the man on air leaned on before reading the dedication." — dedication-reading is the documented core of the format.

### Chicago steppin' — the pack drew the line in the right place

This was the brief's central question for `steppers` and the answer is **yes.**

Steppin' is a **partner** dance: a lead and a follow in a frame, descended from
1930s–40s jitterbug/Lindy through the **bop** (late 50s–60s, Chess and Motown
records), then the "New Bop" of the early 70s, which is the direct foundation of
steppin' as danced now. DJ **Sam Chatman** is credited with coining "steppin'"
in the early 70s. It is counted in **8** (over a six-beat R&B rhythm; some
teachers count 6), it travels, it does not hop, and it is emphatically not a
line dance.

- WTTW News, *From the '40s to Today, A Look at the History of the Chicago Dance Style Known as Steppin'* (27 Feb 2025) — https://news.wttw.com/2025/02/27/40s-today-look-history-chicago-dance-style-known-steppin (journalism of record)
- ABC7 Chicago, *Chicago DJ who coined "Steppin' music" term explains the dance's importance* — https://abc7chicago.com/steppin-music-south-side-chicago-black-history-month-2022/11549403/ (Chatman in his own words)
- The Society of Boppers, Walkers & Steppers, *History* — https://profmilesproduction.com/history/ (community/practitioner)
- Living the 8 Count, *Stepping History* — https://livingthe8count.com/stepping-history/ (community/practitioner)

The pack keeps the two things separate and correctly:

- `steppers / instep / step`: "One count. Left, and the weight moves with you." — **VERIFIED.**
- `steppers / breath / earth`: "Where her weight stays. Nothing bounces, nothing hops, the floor gets all of it." — **VERIFIED**; the no-bounce, weight-in-the-floor quality is exactly what distinguishes steppin' from swing forms it descends from.
- `steppers / posted / opted`: "She chose the partner dance over the line dance and has not looked back." — **VERIFIED**, and this is the clue that does the separating work. Keep it.
- `steppers / duties / duet`: "Two people, one frame, and if it looks like one body moving, it worked." — **VERIFIED**; frame is the correct term for the partner connection.
- `steppers / lowest / west`: "The side of Chicago that gets named first whenever the argument starts." — **VERIFIED as an argument that genuinely exists** (South Side vs. West Side origin claims are live in the community sources above). It asserts only that the argument happens, which it does.

### The line-dance canon (`steppers`)

Only one clue in the pack is about a line dance, and it is right:

- `steppers / listed / slide`: "The one everybody knows: four walls, a quarter turn, and the whole reception joins in." — **VERIFIED.** The Electric Slide is a four-wall dance turning a quarter each sequence. Choreographer Richard L. ### Spades — the rules the pack asserts

The best source here is scholarship written by a player: Amina S. McIntyre,
interviewed in the *American Journal of Play*, vol. 13 nos. 2–3 (2021), pp.
160–167, published by The Strong. It describes Black American spades from the
inside and states the house-rule terminology precisely.

- Amina S. McIntyre, *Reflections on Spades*, American Journal of Play — https://www.museumofplay.org/app/uploads/2022/01/13-2-Article-3-Spades.pdf (scholarship + practitioner)
- Spades glossary, Spades For Money — https://www.spadesformoney.com/spades-glossary.html (community reference; useful for terms, explicit that penalties are house-set)

Verified against those:

- **Book.** "A book is one play in a game where each of the players have thrown out one card; the highest weighted card wins the hand." So `spades / racked / rake` — "To pull the four cards in and stack them face down. Your book." — **VERIFIED**, including the four cards.
- **Leading.** `spades / pleads / leads`: "Whoever took the last book starts the next one. No discussion." — **VERIFIED**: "whoever wins the book throws out the first card."
- **Board / minimum bid.** "Each team must bid at least board (four books)." The pack never uses the word *board*, which is a gap — see MATERIAL WE DON'T HAVE.
- **Renege.** "The one cardinal sin of any Spades hand is reneging" — failing to follow suit while able. `spades / calmed / calmed` ("It took two rounds and a fresh glass of ice water after the renege argument") and `spades / facets / facts` ("who reneged in 2009") are both **VERIFIED** in spirit: the source is explicit that it is treated as cheating and that the accusation is a table event, because "the challenger would have to choose the right book and prove the renege happened."
- **Renege penalty.** "To renege costs you three books or one hundred points if you are caught." Both figures are attested; the glossary is explicit that the exact penalty is house-determined. No clue currently states a number, which is the safe choice. If one ever does, use **three books, or 100 points** and cite McIntyre.
- **Blind bidding.** `spades / racked / dark`: "Bidding blind. Neither of you looked and he said six anyway." — **VERIFIED as terminology and as practice**, with one real sharpening available: at many tables blind bidding is *gated by the score* — "a team can elect to go blind if they are behind one hundred points… If a team wants to go blind seven, it has to be down at least 140 points." A blind six by a team that is *ahead* would be a house-rule violation at those tables. The clue doesn't say who's ahead, so it survives; a future clue could use the gate, which is a much better fact.
- **Nil.** `spades / bailed / idea`: "Bidding nil with the ace of spades still sitting in your hand." — **VERIFIED** as the canonical bad idea. Worth knowing: McIntyre notes nil "is not an option" at some Black American tables at all, which makes the clue table-specific rather than universal. Acceptable.
- **Target score.** `spades / scaled / scale`: "What the whole night is measured on: five hundred, and not one point more." — **VERIFIED.** McIntyre: "the number to reach that is the end of the game (often 250, 300, or 500)."
- **Scoring granularity.** `spades / racked / racked`: "Up two hundred and forty points before the second deck came out." — **VERIFIED as arithmetically possible.** Made books score ten each plus one per bag, so any total ending in a small digit is legal; 240 is a clean made-bid total.
- **The cut.** `spades / facets / fate`: "Whatever the cut hands you." — **VERIFIED**; the opposing player to the dealer's right cuts, and the source treats the cut as a live strategic act.
- **Bags.** `spades / talked / delta`: "The gap between what you bid and what you actually took. Write it small." — **VERIFIED**, and this is the pack's bag clue in all but name. The scorekeeping practice it describes ("write it small", beside the bid) matches McIntyre exactly: "the number over the books bidded is placed beside the original bid number in which case the score could be 128–35."
- **House rules and their enforcer.** `spades / luster / rules`: "House ones. Written nowhere, enforced completely, and she is the authority." — **VERIFIED and well-observed.** McIntyre: "in Black American households there are inscribed rules — informal, customized rules," clarified before play, and "I'm not sure that I ever saw rules to the game written down until I started researching for a class."
- **Table talk.** `spades / played / plea` ("said with the eyebrows only") and `spades / arched / arched` ("One eyebrow, and the bid across the table dropped by two") — **VERIFIED as the correct constraint**: partners cannot table talk, which is precisely why signalling migrates to the face. The source: "Since partners cannot table talk to their partners, trash talking or conversations… will come up in many ways."

### Spades in Black American life

- **Origin and spread.** Descended from bid whist (itself from whist); the family view is that bid whist is "the father of Spades" and that the Black American game is "a hybrid of Bid Whist and Spades." Popular accounts place the game's emergence in 1930s Cincinnati and its national spread through **Black troops in the Second World War** carrying it home from the barracks.
- **HBCUs and campuses.** Spades is transmitted in Black school and campus spaces as much as in homes. McIntyre learned it in a middle-school cafeteria in Atlanta, before homeroom and at lunch; HBCU student organisations still run it as a formal campus institution with tournaments and taught rules.
  - The Nubian Message (NC State), *Card Games and Culture: BSB Spades Workshop* — https://thenubianmessage.com/12230/features/card-games-and-culture-bsb-spades-workshop/ (student press — a campus institution documenting itself)
- **What the table is.** McIntyre's framing is worth the pack's while: spades is "an invited space requiring both an understanding of communal conduct requirements and the game rules"; trash talking is "a version of playing the dozens," goal "to distract, not to isolate or damage."

The WWII-and-Cincinnati origin story is **weakly sourced**. It is repeated
consistently across community writing but I did not find it in primary or
scholarly form, and McIntyre — the best source available — does not assert it.
No clue currently rests on it. **Do not author a clue that asserts it** without
better sourcing.

## WRONG

### 1. The penny-a-record royalty math

> `rnb90s / notice / cent`: "A penny a record after the split, which is what four of them each took home."

**Off by roughly an order of magnitude, and it undersells a real and better
number.** The best-documented 90s R&B case is TLC's, and their deal was the
notoriously bad one: their 1991 LaFace contract paid **56 cents per album sold**,
split three ways — about **19 cents each**, not one cent. They filed Chapter 11
on 3 July 1995 owing $3.5m, having sold in the millions.

The structural point the clue is reaching for is true and is *stronger* than the
penny: 90s artist royalties ran roughly **10–12% of wholesale**, cut by a ~25%
packaging deduction and 3-plus producer points, and paid only after the advance,
video, promotion and tour support recouped — which is why a platinum act can net
zero. Steve Albini's worked example (1993) ends with a band $14,000 *in the hole*
on royalties after selling 250,000 records. Cents-per-record is the right unit;
one cent is not the right number, and "took home" is the wrong verb, because
before recoupment nobody takes home anything.

- Beat Magazine, *The dark tale of TLC going bankrupt in the '90s* — https://beat.com.au/the-dark-tale-of-tlc-going-bankrupt-in-the-90s/ (journalism; 56c and the $3.5m filing)
- AfroTech, *After 5 Years In The Industry, TLC Had Made An Estimated $175M But Said They'd Only Received About 1%* — https://afrotech.com/tlc-bankruptcy (journalism)
- Steve Albini, *The Problem with Music*, The Baffler (1993) — https://thebaffler.com/salvos/the-problem-with-music (primary, contemporaneous, with the arithmetic shown)

### 3. "Powder on a sticky floor"

> `steppers / duties / dust`: "What she shakes onto a sticky gym floor, from a bottle she has had for years."

The author flagged this and was **half right — the practice is real, but the
clue has the physics of the product family backwards, and the community view is
that powder is the thing you shouldn't need.**

What is actually true:

- The commercial product exists and is sold by the pound as **"powdered dance floor wax"** (Lundmark, Triple Crown) — so "powder" and "wax" are not opposites here; the standard product is a powdered wax, applied by sprinkling sparingly onto a clean dry floor. That is the practice the clue is describing and it does happen.
- But within dance communities the distinction is drawn the other way: **powder makes a floor more slippery; wax finish makes it less.** Many established studios forbid both, and floor powder has a reputation as the crutch of dancers "who don't pick up their feet."
- The Chicago stepping community's own answer to a sticky floor is **the shoe, not the floor**: leather or suede soles, "stay away from rubber soles to avoid sticking… or using tons of powder." Which means the pack's *other* shoe clues are the accurate ones, and this clue contradicts them.

- Lundmark Powdered Dance Floor & Shuffleboard Wax — https://www.amazon.com/Lundmark-Wax-LUN-3224P001-Dance-Floor/dp/B002YC21G8 (product; confirms the powdered-wax category and application)
- Dance Forums, *Someone put wax or powder on the Dance floor* — https://www.dance-forums.com/threads/someone-put-wax-or-powder-on-the-dance-floor.50184/ (practitioner discussion; powder-vs-wax direction, and the etiquette objection)
- Cumbe Dance, *Chicago Style Steppin'* — https://www.cumbedance.org/chicago-style-steppin (community; sole guidance)

**Drop-in replacement** for `dust` (answer: DUST; must not contain "dust"):

> `What a rubber sole leaves on a swept floor, and why nobody here wears one.`

(73 chars. Keeps the floor, keeps her authority over it, and now agrees with

**Spades terminology the pack never uses, all sourced to McIntyre:**

- **Board** — the table's minimum team bid, four books. "Each team must bid at least board." The pack has eleven bidding clues and not one uses the word that every table uses.
- **Boston** — taking all the books. McIntyre records it as **"at least ten books won"** constituting a Boston that ends the game outright on the first hand; other sources define it strictly as all thirteen and note "treatment varies." **The pack asserts nothing about Boston, which is the right call given that disagreement** — but if a clue is ever written, it must not state a number as universal.
- **Setting** — failing to make the contract. The pack describes the thing repeatedly and never names it.
- **Cutting** — playing a spade on a trick led in another suit. `spades / scaled / seal` ("the last book taken quietly with a two") is describing a cut with a low trump, and could say so.

**One internal inconsistency to resolve.** `spades / gasped / gasped` puts **the little joker** on the table; `spades / scaled / seal` has **a two** taking the last book. At most tables those two decks are not the same deck — when jokers are in, the twos are typically pulled or re-weighted, and the two of spades is often promoted to high rather than being the quiet low card the `seal` clue depends on. Both clues are individually fine; together they describe two different house rules and a player will notice. Pick a deck.
