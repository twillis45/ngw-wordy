# Black American cultural conversation — snapshot, August 2026

**Compiled 10 August 2026. Shelf life: weeks for §1, months for §2, ~2 years for §3.**

Read `./README.md` first. Nothing in this file may be authored into the 300
evergreen boards in `data/themes.json` without passing the durability test in §7.

## Method and its limits

Per `./README.md`, the citation for discourse is **reporting about the
conversation**, not the conversation itself. No posts were collected, no
screenshots taken, and no private individual's activity was compiled. Where a
person appears here they are a public figure acting publicly — an athlete in a
game, an artist on a stage, an award recipient.

That boundary has a cost worth stating plainly: **§1 is the weakest section in
this file.** "What Black Twitter is talking about this week" is precisely the
thing that only exists as the posts themselves, and reporting about a given week
lags the week. What follows in §1 is what has published coverage, not a claim to
have taken the temperature of a room.

## 3. Structural — where the conversation actually lives (most important section)

### The finding: it did not move, it decomposed

The consensus of reporting and scholarship is that **no single platform inherited
Black Twitter's role**, and that framing the question as "where did it go?"
misreads what it was.

**Rianna Walcott, "#RIP Twitter: The Conditions of Black Social Media Platform
Migration," Just Tech / SSRC** is the best single field review.
<https://just-tech.ssrc.org/field-reviews/rip-twitter-the-conditions-of-black-social-media-platform-migration/>
Her argument: Black Twitter was a "heterogeneous Black discourse collective" that
*co-designed* the platform — the hashtag, the @-mention and the retweet are
user inventions Twitter absorbed — and its value came from Twitter's position at
the **centre of a cross-platform hypernarrative**, not from Twitter itself. She
calls the movement outward *transmigration*: moving across a space while changing
it. Post-2022 changes (paid verification, reach throttling, rate limits) degraded
the connective function specifically, which is why fragmentation followed rather
than relocation.

The standard scholarship she builds on, and the standard for this repo:

- **André Brock, Jr., *Distributed Blackness: African American Cybercultures***
  (NYU Press, 2020) — the foundational text; Black technology use read from
  Black perspectives rather than as deviation from a white default.
  <https://nyupress.org/9781479820375/distributed-blackness/>
- **Sarah Florini, *Beyond Hashtags: Racial Politics and Black Digital Networks***
  (NYU Press, 2019) — argues the network was **already multi-platform and
  multi-modal** (podcasts, This Week in Blackness, Twitter) well before the X
  exodus. This matters enormously: it means 2022–2025 did not *create*
  fragmentation, it removed the one hub that had made a distributed network look
  centralised.
  <https://nyupress.org/9781479813056/beyond-hashtags/>

The academic framing of the post-Twitter landscape generally:
Tkacz & Gehl, "After Twitter: Fragmentation, Platform Polities and Protective
Sociality," *Social Media + Society* (2025).
<https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/20563051251366907>

### Where people actually went

- **X** — still holds a share, and reporting has consistently declined to declare
  it dead. Prominent departures (Gabrielle Union, Don Lemon among the named
  public figures) were reported as the visible edge of a broader exodus.
  <https://www.nbcnews.com/news/nbcblk/black-twitter-x-elon-musk-exodus-bluesky-rcna180147>
- **Bluesky / Blacksky** — the most structurally interesting outcome.
  **Blacksky** (founder Rudy Fraser; launched 19 June 2023 — Juneteenth, and the
  date is not incidental) is not a separate app but **independent infrastructure
  on the AT Protocol**: its own feeds, its own moderation service, its own
  labelling. It reached **750,000+ users without paid promotion**, and scaled
  moderation from ~10 reports/week to **500+/day**, with dedicated filters for
  **misogynoir** and anti-Black harassment handled by Black moderators who can
  act faster than Bluesky's central team.
  - <https://nonprofitquarterly.org/building-blacksky/>
  - <https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/rudy-fraser> (Berkman Klein)
  - <https://blackskyweb.xyz/blog/> (primary)

  The reason this is the section's key finding: Blacksky is the first serious
  answer to "who moderates us" that isn't "petition the platform." Whether it
  becomes *the* successor is unsettled and I would not claim it does.
- **Threads** — largest raw destination; analysts project it passing X on monthly
  actives during 2026. Weak for real-time discourse by design.
- **TikTok and Instagram** — where the *vernacular* actually propagates now, even
  where the argument does not. This is the crucial split: **commentary and
  breaking-news discourse fragmented across text platforms, while slang, dance,
  sound and format diffusion consolidated onto short-form video.** Any assumption
  that "Black Twitter" is still primarily a text phenomenon is out of date.
- **Spill** — Black-owned, founded by ex-Twitter staff; persistently named in
  coverage, but I found no 2026 reporting establishing scale. Treat as
  unverified for size.

### What to distrust in this section

Most "Twitter alternatives 2026" material surfacing in search is SEO content with
no named author or editorial accountability (axis-intelligence, getskyscraper,
posteverywhere, netinfluencer). **None of it is cited above and none of it should
be.** User-count figures for Bluesky in particular vary wildly across those pages;
where a number matters, get it from Bluesky or from a named reporter.

## 4. Vocabulary — and the absorption problem

The single most useful finding for this repo:

**Ilbury, C. (2026), "'Gen Z Language? Y'all Mean AAVE': The Appropriation of
African American Vernacular English as 'TikTok Language'," *Journal of
Sociolinguistics*.**
<https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/josl.70024>

The mechanism it documents, which is exactly the thing a word game gets wrong:

1. An AAVE feature diffuses via short-form video.
2. It is relabelled **"Gen Z slang" or "internet slang"** — a label that strips
   attribution while preserving the cultural cachet.
3. Non-Black speakers extract value from it and **drop it when the value
   expires**, at no cost.
4. Black speakers who keep using it continue to face linguistic discrimination
   for the same forms.

Ilbury names *finna*, *no cap*, *bussin* and *slaps* as terms now presented as
origin-neutral. Add to that, on the same reasoning and with the same caution:
*bae, lit, woke, on fleek, salty, shade, tea, periodt, ate, slay, rizz* (the last
via a different but comparable diffusion path).

### The authoring rule this implies

**A term that has completed absorption is no longer usable as a Black-culture
clue — and using it as one is the specific failure mode to avoid.** Cluing *SLAY*
or *TEA* as Black vernacular in 2026 reads to a Black player as the game learning
the word from a brand account. Cluing it as generic internet slang erases the
origin. There is no good move; the move is not to use it.

The durable material is not the absorbed vocabulary but **the terms that carry
their own history and resist relabelling**: *signifying*, *the dozens*, *code-
switching*, *misogynoir* (Moya Bailey's coinage, and Blacksky's use of it as a
live moderation category in 2026 is evidence it is load-bearing, not academic
jargon), *kitchen* (the nape hairline), *Sunday best*, *cookout*. Those have
lineage a clue can cite. Absorbed slang has only a moment.

Genuinely new 2026-specific coinages: **I did not find any documented in
accountable reporting.** Slang glossaries published this year are largely
recycled and largely the absorbed set above. Reporting that a word is "new this
year" is usually reporting that a journalist encountered it this year. I am
recording this as a null finding rather than filling the gap.

## 7. Durability assessment — how much of this can a word game actually use?

The test: **would this clue still read correctly to a player in 2031, with no
knowledge of 2026?** Not "would it still be true" — a stale clue and a broken
clue are indistinguishable from the player's side.

### Durable enough to author against (evergreen-eligible)

- **Nothing here, unconditionally.** But two items are close:
  - ***Sinners*, Ryan Coogler, Michael B. Jordan, 2025 film, Oscar record.** An
    awards record is a permanent fact and a film enters the canon or doesn't. A
    clue phrased around the *film and its makers* survives; a clue phrased
    around "this year's big movie" does not. Even so, it is five years of
    hindsight away from being canon, and I would hold it out of the 300 until
    it has that.
  - **The vocabulary *rule* in §4** — not the words, the rule. "Don't clue
    absorbed slang as Black-coded" is a permanent authoring constraint and
    should be promoted out of `current/` into the authoring contract. That is
    the highest-value output in this file.

### Already decaying (rotating pack only, or nothing)

- Everything in §1. It is a calendar and it expires this month.
- Platform geography (§3). This is the fastest-decaying section in the file and
  the README predicted it would be: a clue naming Bluesky, Threads or Blacksky
  is a bet on a 2026 market position. Blacksky's *existence and premise* —
  community-run moderation infrastructure on open protocol — may prove durable;
  its user count certainly will not.
- Grammy and Super Bowl outcomes. True forever, referenced for about eighteen
  months. A 2031 player reading a Super Bowl LX halftime clue has to do date
  arithmetic to understand why it was ever notable.
- WNBA CBA. Superseded by the next CBA, by definition.
- The absorbed-slang list. Decaying in the opposite direction — those words are
  drifting *further* from Black-coded every year, so a 2031 player is even less
  likely to read the clue as intended than a 2026 one.

### The load-bearing conclusion

**This research supports roughly one authoring decision and zero clues.** The
decision is §4's rule. Everything else belongs in a dated rotating pack that the
product has not committed to building, and per `./README.md` that commitment is
a product decision, not a research one. If the rotating pack is never built,
the correct use of this file is: read the vocabulary section, apply the rule,
ship nothing from the rest.
