# Eyes on the Prize — the corpus and its chronology

Working note, not canon. Written to the standard in `docs/research/README.md`:
every claim carries a URL, primary and archival first.

**Copyright boundary.** The two series and all three companion books are in
copyright. Nothing here transcribes, excerpts or paraphrases-at-length any of
them. What this file carries is what copyright does not reach: dates, names,
places, event sequence, and bibliographic structure. The one place a phrase is
quoted — the Kerner Commission's "two societies" — is a US government report,
quoted at four words, because the wording is the reason an episode is titled
what it is.

## 1. The corpus, described

### 1.1 The films

Produced by **Blackside, Inc.** (Boston), founded by **Henry Hampton**
(1940–1998), narrated by **Julian Bond**. Fourteen hours in two series.

**Eyes on the Prize: America's Civil Rights Years, 1954–1965** — PBS, six
episodes, Wednesdays, 21 Jan – 25 Feb 1987.

| # | Episode | Period | First aired | What it covers |
|---|---------|--------|-------------|----------------|
| 1 | Awakenings | 1954–1956 | 21 Jan 1987 | Emmett Till's murder and the Bryant/Milam trial; the Montgomery bus boycott |
| 2 | Fighting Back | 1957–1962 | 28 Jan 1987 | *Brown* and massive resistance; Little Rock Central High; James Meredith at Ole Miss |
| 3 | Ain't Scared of Your Jails | 1960–1961 | 4 Feb 1987 | Greensboro and the sit-in wave; founding of SNCC; the Freedom Rides |
| 4 | No Easy Walk | 1962–1963 | 11 Feb 1987 | Albany; Birmingham; the March on Washington |
| 5 | Mississippi: Is This America? | 1962–1964 | 18 Feb 1987 | Mississippi voter registration, Freedom Summer, the MFDP challenge |
| 6 | Bridge to Freedom | 1965 | 25 Feb 1987 | Selma, Bloody Sunday, the march to Montgomery, the Voting Rights Act |

**Eyes on the Prize II: America at the Racial Crossroads, 1965–1985** — PBS,
eight episodes, 15 Jan – 5 Mar 1990.

| # | Episode | Period | First aired | What it covers |
|---|---------|--------|-------------|----------------|
| 7 | The Time Has Come | 1964–1966 | 15 Jan 1990 | Malcolm X; the Meredith March; the Black Power turn in SNCC |
| 8 | Two Societies | 1965–1968 | 22 Jan 1990 | Chicago Freedom Movement; Detroit 1967; the Kerner Report |
| 9 | Power! | 1966–1968 | 29 Jan 1990 | Carl Stokes in Cleveland; the Black Panther Party; Ocean Hill–Brownsville |
| 10 | The Promised Land | 1967–1968 | 5 Feb 1990 | King's anti-war turn, the Memphis strike, the assassination, the Poor People's Campaign |

Sources for the episode list, order and dates: episode-guide listing of titles
and original air dates, <https://epguides.com/EyesonthePrize/>; series and
episode framing on PBS American Experience,
<https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/americanexperience/films/eyesontheprize/>; the AAPB
production history of the series (which episodes were assigned to which
producer/director), <https://americanarchive.org/exhibits/eotp/2-making-television-history>.
Air dates in secondary episode guides are the weakest link in this file; the
episode **titles and period subtitles** are corroborated across PBS, AAPB and
library catalogue records, but treat a specific 1990 Monday/Wednesday air date
as good-not-gold until checked against a PBS programme log.

**Eyes on the Prize III: We Who Believe in Freedom Cannot Rest** (2025) is a
later continuation under the same title, not part of the Hampton/Blackside
fourteen. Do not fold it into the 1987/1990 numbering.

### 1.2 The companion books

1. **Juan Williams, *Eyes on the Prize: America's Civil Rights Years,
   1954–1965*.** New York: Viking, 1987. ISBN 0-670-81412-1. Penguin paperback
   1988, ISBN 0-14-009653-1; Penguin reissue 2013 with an introduction by
   Julian Bond, ISBN 978-0-14-312474-0. **A narrative history** written to
   accompany series I — continuous prose by a single author, roughly 300 pages,
   heavily illustrated with the series' photographic research.
   Editions: <https://openlibrary.org/search?q=eyes+on+the+prize+juan+williams>;
   1987 Viking printing scanned at <https://archive.org/details/eyesonprizeameri00will>.

2. **Clayborne Carson, David J. Garrow, Gerald Gill, Vincent Harding and
   Darlene Clark Hine (general editors), *The Eyes on the Prize Civil Rights
   Reader: Documents, Speeches, and Firsthand Accounts from the Black Freedom
   Struggle, 1954–1990*.** New York: Penguin, 1991. ISBN 0-14-015403-5 /
   978-0-14-015403-0. **A documentary reader** — court opinions, organisational
   statements, speeches and contemporaneous accounts, edited and introduced,
   not a narrative. Publisher record:
   <https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/323119/the-eyes-on-the-prize-civil-rights-reader-by-general-editors-clayborne-carson-david-j-garrow-gerald-gill-vincent-harding-darlene-clark-hine/>.
   Note the terminal date: **1990**, not 1985 — the reader runs past the films.

3. **Henry Hampton and Steve Fayer, with Sarah Flynn, *Voices of Freedom: An
   Oral History of the Civil Rights Movement from the 1950s Through the
   1980s*.** New York: Bantam, 1990. ISBN 0-553-05734-0 (hardcover);
   0-553-35232-6 / 978-0-553-35232-0 (trade paperback, ~692 pp.).
   **An oral history**, assembled from the interviews Blackside recorded for
   the two series — the material that did not fit into fourteen hours.
   <https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/74526/voices-of-freedom-by-henry-hampton-and-steve-fayer/>.

Practical consequence for this repo: of the three, only the **reader** is a
route to primary documents, and its documents are almost all separately
available from LOC/NARA. **Cite the primary document, never the reader's
printing of it.** *Voices of Freedom* is the one book whose value is
irreducibly its own text — which is exactly the book we must not quote from.

### 1.3 Why so little of this is accessible

The films were licensed the way 1980s documentaries were licensed: term-limited
music and archival-footage clearances. Roughly **130 copyrighted songs** sit in
the fourteen hours. PBS's broadcast rights ended in **1993**, most licences had
lapsed by **1995**, the VHS sets went out of print, and Blackside could not
fund renewal — the series became functionally unavailable for about a decade,
with used VHS sets trading near $1,000 by January 2005.

A **Ford Foundation** grant of $65,000 funded the assessment; Ford and the
Gilder Foundation then funded re-clearance, led by music supervisor Rena
Kosersky, with the goal of clearing in perpetuity. **Series I** was re-cleared
in 2005, rebroadcast, and issued on DVD; **Series II** was judged too expensive
to clear for commercial release and stayed restricted to educational
distribution well past that.

- DOC NYC case study, with the cost figures and timeline:
  <https://www.docnyc.net/news/case-study-on-savedocs-eyes-on-the-prize/>
- Center for Media and Social Impact, *Untold Stories* — the report that made
  this the standard example of clearance-culture damage:
  <https://cmsimpact.org/resource/untold_stories/>
- Contemporaneous coverage, Jan–Feb 2005: *Washington Post*,
  <https://www.washingtonpost.com/wp-dyn/articles/A14801-2005Jan16_2.html>;
  Democracy Now!, <https://www.democracynow.org/2005/2/8/copyright_issues_block_broadcast_of_award>

This is the reason a researcher hits the **interview outtakes** before the
episodes: the raw interviews were preserved and digitised on separate money and
are open, while the finished films were locked up.

## 2. Milestone chronology, 1954–1985

Cited by cluster; the cluster citation follows each block.

### Brown, Till, Montgomery (1954–1956) — Episode 1–2 material

| Date | Event | Place | Why it mattered |
|---|---|---|---|
| 17 May 1954 | *Brown v. Board of Education* decided, 9–0 | Washington, DC | Separate educational facilities held inherently unequal; overturns *Plessy* in schooling |
| 31 May 1955 | *Brown II* | Washington, DC | Remands desegregation to district courts "with all deliberate speed" — the phrase that licensed a decade of delay |
| 28 Aug 1955 | Emmett Till, 14, abducted and murdered | Money / Drew, Mississippi | Mamie Till-Mobley's open-casket decision made the killing national; the photographs radicalised a generation of Black teenagers |
| 23 Sep 1955 | Bryant and Milam acquitted | Sumner, Mississippi | An all-white jury deliberated about an hour; the two later described the killing to *Look* magazine, unpunishable |
| 1 Dec 1955 | Rosa Parks arrested | Montgomery, Alabama | Parks was NAACP branch secretary and a trained organiser, not an accidental passenger |
| 5 Dec 1955 | Boycott begins; Montgomery Improvement Association formed, King elected president | Montgomery | Puts a 26-year-old pastor at the head of a mass movement |
| 13 Nov 1956 | Supreme Court affirms *Browder v. Gayle* | Washington, DC | Bus segregation unconstitutional — the legal win the boycott was built to force |
| 20–21 Dec 1956 | Order served; buses desegregated; boycott ends after 381 days | Montgomery | Proves sustained mass economic non-cooperation works |

Sources: *Brown*, NARA's page for the decision and the digitised opinion,
<https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/brown-v-board-of-education>;
Till, National Park Service, Emmett Till and Mamie Till-Mobley National
Monument, <https://www.nps.gov/emti/index.htm>, and the FBI's 2006 prosecutive
report, <https://vault.fbi.gov/Emmett%20Till%20>; Montgomery, King Institute at
Stanford, <https://kinginstitute.stanford.edu/montgomery-bus-boycott> and
<https://kinginstitute.stanford.edu/browder-v-gayle-352-us-903>; Parks,
Library of Congress Rosa Parks Papers, <https://www.loc.gov/collections/rosa-parks-papers/>.

## 3. Where the archives are

video elements, news footage (in some cases the only surviving copy), interview
audio and video, transcripts, research files, correspondence.

- Collection overview: <https://library.washu.edu/spec/henry-hampton-collection/>

- Archive blog, *Out of the Archive*: <https://wufilmarchive.wordpress.com/>

**American Archive of Public Broadcasting (LOC + GBH) — Eyes on the Prize
Interviews.** Over 300 raw interviews with full transcripts, keyword- and
name-searchable, preserved 2010–2016 with Mellon and NHPRC funding and
contributed from 2016. This is the single most useful open door into the corpus.

- Interviews: <https://americanarchive.org/special_collections/eotp-interviews>
- Exhibit, including a timeline and a map: <https://americanarchive.org/exhibits/eotp>

**Library of Congress.**

**National Archives and Records Administration.**

## 4. What this grounds in the game

`data/themes.json` ships 15 themes; the ones this chronology touches are
**juneteenth (The Nineteenth)**, **hbcu**, **church (Sunday Service)** and
**reunion (Family Reunion)**. `data/canon.json` currently holds **zero**
entries, so nothing below conflicts with an existing citation — these are
candidates for the first entries.

### Nothing here contradicts a shipped clue.

I checked every clue in those four themes carrying a date, a place-name or a
legal/political reference against the chronology above. No factual conflict.
Specifically checked and **consistent**:

- **juneteenth / `hard`** — "What thirty months of being kept in the dark was,
  and no part of the day pretends otherwise." Emancipation Proclamation
  1 Jan 1863 → Galveston 19 Jun 1865 is 29.6 months. "Thirty months" is right
  as spoken rounding.
- **juneteenth / `depart` pack** — "Whose it was to walk from Fort Worth toward
  Washington at eighty-nine, and keep walking until it passed." Opal Lee, born
  7 Oct 1926, began the symbolic walks in 2016 at 89; Juneteenth became the
  eleventh federal holiday when S.475 was signed 17 Jun 2021.
  <https://nmaahc.si.edu/explore/stories/who-opal-lee>
- **reunion** — "The Chicago side has it this year, and the program lists all
  six in the order they went north." Great Migration framing; consistent with
  the northern-migration chronology and with Episode 8's Chicago material.

### One pre-existing flag this research reinforces (not new)

- **juneteenth blurb** ("A paper read out loud on a Galveston balcony") and
  **juneteenth / `herald`** ("a man on a balcony with somebody else's paper in
  his hand"). The balcony is traditional, not documented; General Order No. 3
  was posted and read at several sites in Galveston. `docs/research/CANON.md`
  already uses this exact case as its worked example, so the finding is known —
  but the **blurb** states the balcony flatly, where the clue at least frames it
  as what the word meant. Worth deciding whether the blurb should soften.
  Primary text of the order: <https://www.archives.gov/news/articles/juneteenth-original-document>

### Material we don't have — sourced, and worth authoring toward

These are the strongest untouched hooks the chronology offers, all verifiable
from the archives in §3:

- **HBCU.** The sit-in wave was HBCU students: four **North Carolina A&T**
  freshmen on 1 Feb 1960, and **SNCC founded at Shaw University**, Raleigh,
  15–17 Apr 1960 — a Black college hosting the founding, with Ella Baker
  insisting the students stay independent. The HBCU pack currently has nothing
  about the yard as a political base. <https://snccdigital.org/events/>

### Tone — needs a human

Nothing in §2 settles how any of this should *sound* in a word game. A pack
about a cookout that reaches for Birmingham has a tonal problem research cannot
adjudicate, and the four-girls bombing in particular is a place where "a factual
claim wearing a joke" is the wrong instrument. That is a reader's call, and it
stays open.
