# The Shop — grounding research for `barbershop` and `salon`

Covers the two packs in `data/themes.json` whose id is `barbershop` (14 boards)
and `salon` (24 boards). Written to the standard in `docs/research/README.md`.

Structural note the bench asked for up front: **Barbershop shipped and the salon
did not exist**, so "the shop" was male by default across the whole product. The
salon pack now exists and is the larger of the two. The most valuable finding
below is not a correction — it is the **MATERIAL WE DON'T HAVE** section, which
is where the salon's own institutional history sits, and the pack currently does
not touch it.

## VERIFIED

### The conk — the history, not the tone

**Clue** — theme `barbershop`, base `conked`, row `conked`:
> "How the old heads wore it before the fade, lye and all, and it burned."

Every factual element checks out.

- **The name.** "Conk" is a shortening of **congolene**, the lye-based gel used
  to straighten hair. Popular among Black men from roughly the **1920s to the
  1960s**.
- **The process.** The relaxer was frequently mixed at home from **lye, eggs and
  potatoes**; the applier wore gloves and the head had to be rinsed fast to stop
  the chemical burn. The potato was in the mix to blunt the burn — it did not
  stop it.
- **"and it burned"** is the literal, documented experience, not a flourish.
- **Malcolm X's account.** *The Autobiography of Malcolm X* (with Alex Haley,
  1965) devotes a passage — anthologised as "My First Conk" — to his first conk,
  in Roxbury, Massachusetts, around 1941, at sixteen, applied by his friend
  Shorty. His conclusion is the line the clue is standing next to:
  > "This was my first really big step toward self-degradation."
  He read it as Black self-denial in imitation of a white aesthetic, and
  repudiated it after joining the Nation of Islam in 1952.
- **Why it fell away.** The conk did not fade for fashion reasons alone. It was
  displaced through the 1960s by the natural / Afro, carrying exactly the
  political reading Malcolm X had given it.

Sources:
- [Malcolm X, "Hair" / "My First Conk", excerpted from *The Autobiography of Malcolm X* (Fairfax County Public Schools PDF)](https://onlinecampus.fcps.edu/media2/English/FCPS_Current_Courses/English_10/English_10_Unit_5_Fences/referdocs/Hair.pdf)
- [Byrd & Tharps, *Hair Story: Untangling the Roots of Black Hair in America*, St. Martin's, rev. ed. 2014](https://www.amazon.com/Hair-Story-Untangling-Roots-America/dp/1250046572) — the standard reference; covers congolene, the hot comb, press-and-curl, and the Afro's displacement of the conk.

**The clue is factually clean. The tone question is NOT settled by this
research — see TONE below.**

### The barbershop as a Black institution

The pack's premise — that the shop is a public room, not a service counter — is
the settled scholarly position, and there are two primary works to cite rather
than gesture at.

- **Quincy T. Mills, *Cutting Along the Color Line: Black Barbers and Barber
  Shops in America*, University of Pennsylvania Press, 2013.** Traces the trade
  1820–1970. Two findings the pack should know: (1) nineteenth-century Black
  barbers were economically mobile but were **obliged by custom to serve an
  exclusively white clientele** — the Black barbershop as a *Black* room is a
  later thing; (2) between roughly **1890 and 1970**, via the "new negro"
  barbers, the Great Migration and the civil rights movement, Black-owned shops
  were converted into Black commercial **and public** space. Mills is Associate
  Professor of History, Vassar College.
  [Publisher](https://www.pennpress.org/9780812245417/cutting-along-the-color-line/) ·
  [JSTOR](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctt4cghgr)
- **Melissa Harris-Perry (as Melissa Harris-Lacewell), *Barbershops, Bibles, and
  BET: Everyday Talk and Black Political Thought*, Princeton University Press,
  2004.** The primary work — this is the one to cite, not the TV persona. Argues
  Black political ideology is formed in **everyday talk in ordinary public
  spaces**, barbershops among them, rather than handed down by national elites.
  Co-winner, 2005 W.E.B. Du Bois Book Award, National Conference of Black
  Political Scientists.
  [Internet Archive (full text, borrowable)](https://archive.org/details/barbershopsbible0000harr)

This directly vouches for the pack's `argued` board ("nobody in it wanted to
win, just to keep going") and `combat` ("what the Friday argument sounds like
from the sidewalk and never actually is"). The argument *is* the institution.
That is Harris-Perry's thesis in one clue.

### Licensing history — the shop is regulated, and the regulation has a race history

The pack's `permit` board ("Framed crooked over the mirror, her name on it,
renewed every two years") is sitting on real ground, and there is more under it
than the pack uses.

- First barber licensing laws date from the **1880s**, tightening after.
- The **Journeymen Barbers International Union of America** pushed sanitation
  licensing as a competitive weapon, with the explicit effect of excluding Black
  barbers from first-class shops. Many barber schools excluded Black men, so
  Black entrepreneurs founded their own schools to get students licensed.
- **Alabama's cosmetology examinations remained segregated until 1971** —
  separate "colored" and "white" examination results recorded 1946–1970.
  [Alabama Dept. of Archives & History, cosmetology and barbering ledgers](https://fortherecordalabama.blog/2022/12/06/cosmetology-and-barbering-ledgers/) ·
  ["Regulating Beauty: The Licensing of Barbers and Beauticians in Alabama and the Nation", *Enterprise & Society*, Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/enterprise-and-society/article/regulating-beauty-the-licensing-of-barbers-and-beauticians-in-alabama-and-the-nation/69A7A5E320A13E01E7192699B6AC6E4E)

### Guard numbers and the line-up — is the pack using it the way barbers do?

Yes. Checked against barber-facing sources.

- **The numbering is standardised across Wahl, Andis and BaByliss**, so a
  "number two" means the same thing whoever is holding the clippers. `#1` ≈ 1/8
  in., **`#2` ≈ 1/4 in. (6 mm)**, `#4` ≈ 1/2 in.
- Clue `argued`/`guard` — "Number two on the clippers" — **correct**.
- Clue `number`/`number` — "Three on the sides, one on top" — **correct usage**;
  that is how the length is spoken, and a #1 top over #3 sides is a real cut.
- **Fade vs. taper**: a taper shortens only around the neckline and sideburns; a
  fade blends much higher and is a more dramatic transition. Clue
  `fabled`/`fade` — "Skin at the bottom, full at the top, and the whole art is
  the part between" — **correct**, and correctly identifies the blend as the
  skill.
- **Line-up / edge-up** are the trade terms for the clean work at the hairline
  and perimeter. Clues `nickel`/`line` ("Straight across the forehead"),
  `carpet`/`trace` and `shaved`/`save` ("What the line-up did for that photo")
  all use it as barbers do.
- One thing the pack does **not** have and could use: the **taper lever** on the
  clipper, which moves the cutting blade against the fixed blade for about a
  **1/16-inch** adjustment without changing guards. That is the barber's fine
  control, and it is invisible to the client.

Sources:
- [StyleSeat Pro, haircut number system](https://www.styleseat.com/blog/haircut-number-system/)
- [Sacred Barbers, clipper guard numbers](https://sacredbarbers.com/blog/clipper-guard-numbers)
- [BuyBarber, clipper guard size chart](https://buybarber.com/blogs/news/clipper-guard-sizes-chart)

### Booth rental — the pack asserts a stylist rents her own chair

**Clue** — theme `salon`, base `rental`, row `rental`:
> "Two hundred a week for the station by the window, paid to the woman up front."

**Verified, and the number is right.** Booth rental is one of the two dominant
models (the other being commission), and the renter is an **independent
contractor (1099)** who buys her own product, carries her own insurance, does
her own marketing, books and taxes, and keeps 100% of the service ticket.

- Weekly booth rent commonly runs **$150–$300**, i.e. the clue's "two hundred a
  week" sits mid-range. Monthly figures: **$400–$600** typical market,
  **$800–$1,600** mid-metro, **$1,500–$2,500** in NYC/LA/Boston.
- Commission by contrast is typically a **40–60% split** of service revenue,
  often with product cost deducted, plus ~10–15% on retail.
- "Paid to the woman up front" is the correct mechanic: the renter pays the
  **salon owner**, who is a landlord in that relationship, not an employer. This
  is why `rental`/`rent` ("What she quit paying somebody else the year she
  signed her own lease") and `staked`/`staked` work — they describe the move from
  renter to owner, which is the actual career ladder in this trade.
- Sources: [Vagaro, commission vs. booth rental](https://www.vagaro.com/learn/salon-commission-vs-booth-rental-guide) ·
  [Booksy, employee vs booth renter vs independent contractor](https://biz.booksy.com/en-us/blog/employee-vs-booth-renter-vs-independent-contractor-key-differences-in-the-salon-industry) ·
  [StyleSeat, booth rental vs commission](https://www.styleseat.com/blog/booth-rental-vs-commission-salons/)

### Wash day, and the length of an appointment

**Clues** — theme `salon`, base `waters`, row `waters`:
> "What wash day runs through, gallon after gallon, from ten until noon."

and base `entail`, row `entail`:
> "What just a trim actually means: two hours, a wash, and a whole conversation."

and base `streak`, row `take`:
> "How long it really is. She said all day on the phone and she meant it."

**Verified, with a caveat on wash day.** Wash day is a named, recognised ritual
— pre-wash and detangle, wash, deep condition, leave-in and oil, then style.
Practitioner sources put the **wash portion at 30–90 minutes** for most people
depending on density and length; the **hours** come from the styling that
follows, and the whole day framing is real when styling is included. So the
clue's "ten until noon" is defensible for a full wash-and-style but is on the
long side for the washing alone.

**Braid appointments: 5–8 hours is the documented range**, so the pack's
six-hour figure and `insert`/`rinse` ("the one minute in six hours when nobody
talks") are well within it.

Sources: [CNN, "In photos: 'Wash Day' honors a classic Black hair ritual"](https://edition.cnn.com/2024/04/19/style/wash-day-photos-black-hair-tomesha-faxio) ·
[Black Curl Magic, wash day step timing](https://www.blackcurlmagic.com/blog/how-naturals-can-turn-wash-day-into-wash-hour) ·
[Black Hair Information, "How long is too long to spend in the hair salon?"](https://blackhairinformation.com/general-articles/opinion/general-opinion/long-long-spend-hair-salon/)

### The relaxer is not an acid

**Clue** — theme `salon`, base `placid`, row `acid`:
> "What a relaxer is not, though your grandmother will call it that anyway."

**Verified and unusually sharp.** A relaxer is **alkaline** — lye relaxers are
sodium hydroxide; "no-lye" formulas are calcium/guanidine hydroxide. Chemically
the opposite of an acid. The colloquial "the acid" or "a perm" persisting
regardless is exactly the generational-vocabulary point the clue is making, and
it ties straight back to the conk: same chemical family, same burn.
(Byrd & Tharps, *Hair Story*, chapters on straightening chemistry.)

### The Dominican blowout

**Clue** — theme `salon`, base `latino`, row `latino`:
> "The Dominican shop two blocks over, where a blowout runs an hour and costs less."

**Verified.** Dominican salons cluster in Washington Heights, the Bronx, Harlem,
Queens and parts of Brooklyn. A wash-and-set runs from about **$15**, a wash /
deep condition / blowout package around **$25**, and the visit is commonly
**about 1.5 hours, walk-in, no appointment**. "Runs an hour" is slightly quick
but in range; "costs less" is correct against a standard salon ticket.
[Fashionista, "Five reasons why you should be getting a Dominican-style blowout"](https://fashionista.com/2013/07/five-reasons-why-you-should-be-getting-a-dominican-style-blowout)

### Madam C.J. Walker and the economic independence argument

The `salon` pack's owner-operator spine is historically load-bearing, and this
is its origin.

- Walker trained agents and "beauty culturists" from **1906**, opened **Lelia
  College of Beauty Culture** in Pittsburgh in **1908**, moved to Indianapolis by
  **1910** and built a factory, salon and second training school.
- She organised agents into state and local clubs, forming the **National Beauty
  Culturists and Benevolent Association of Madam C.J. Walker Agents**, and held
  her **first national agents' convention in Philadelphia in 1917**, by which
  point she claimed to have trained nearly **20,000** former maids, cooks,
  teachers and farm labourers.
- The argument is explicitly economic, not cosmetic: she taught agents to
  **budget, run a business and become financially independent**, and the network
  reached roughly **40,000** people across the US, Central America and the
  Caribbean.
- **Annie Turnbo Malone** precedes and parallels her and should be named
  alongside — the pack currently names neither.

Sources: [National Women's History Museum](https://www.womenshistory.org/education-resources/biographies/madam-cj-walker) ·
[Encyclopedia of Indianapolis](https://indyencyclopedia.org/madam-c-j-walker/) ·
[Library of Congress, *Headlines & Heroes*](https://blogs.loc.gov/headlinesandheroes/2020/05/madam-c-j-walker/) ·
Byrd & Tharps, *Hair Story*.

### The CROWN Act — the live legal fact

The salon pack touches hair-as-politics (`spinal`/`plain`, `streak`/`streak`,
`trends`) without ever naming the law. Current status, as of this research:

- **CROWN** = **C**reating a **R**espectful and **O**pen **W**orld for Natural
  **H**air. Bans discrimination on hair texture and protective styles in
  employment and education.
- **California was first: signed by Gov. Newsom on 3 July 2019.**
- **It is NOT federal law.** The House passed it 235–189 in March 2022 (a
  further House passage is cited for 2023); it has never cleared the Senate. It
  was **reintroduced on 26 February 2025** as **H.R. 1638** (Rep. Bonnie Watson
  Coleman, D-NJ) and **S. 751** (Sen. Cory Booker, D-NJ).
- **Roughly 30 states** have enacted a CROWN Act or equivalent, including CA,
  NY, NJ, VA, CO, WA, MD, DE, NV, OR, ME, NE, TN, MA, MN, CT and NM, plus city
  ordinances such as Philadelphia. The count has moved upward year on year — any
  clue that states a number will go stale, so **do not put the number in a
  clue.**

Sources: [The CROWN Act coalition](https://www.thecrownact.com/about) ·
[NAACP, The CROWN Act](https://naacp.org/resources/crown-act-creating-respectful-and-open-workplace-natural-hair) ·
[NAACP Legal Defense Fund, hair discrimination FAQ](https://www.naacpldf.org/natural-hair-discrimination/) ·
[Jackson Lewis, House passage](https://www.jacksonlewis.com/insights/house-passes-crown-act-ban-natural-hair-discrimination-workplace)

## TONE — NEEDS A HUMAN

1. **The conk clue.** Everything in it is true. The bench's actual question —
   *does "lye and all, and it burned" read as respect for the old heads or as
   novelty at their expense?* — **is not answered by anything above, and this
   research does not settle it.** Malcolm X's own conclusion is that the conk was
   self-degradation; a clue that treats it as period colour is standing on top of
   that conclusion whether or not it means to. The strongest thing research can
   say is: the history is documented, the judgement was made by the man who wore
   it, and whether the clue's register honours or trivialises that is a reader's
   call. **Stays open. Stays flagged.**
2. **`barbershop` / `fabled` / `deaf`** — "The old head in the corner is nearly
   it, which is why he talks the loudest." Affectionate in intent; a disability
   read is available. Not a research question.
3. **`salon` / `preach` / `preach`** — "She is going to tell you about that man
   again, and she is not wrong." Whether this lands as the room's own humour or
   as a stereotype about Black women's talk is exactly the kind of thing a
   citation cannot decide.
4. **`salon` / `placid` / `acid`** — factually excellent, but "your grandmother
   will call it that anyway" positions the elder as the one who is wrong. Worth
   a reader's eye on the register, not the fact.

## MATERIAL WE DON'T HAVE

**This is the section the bench asked for, and it is the most valuable finding
here.** The salon pack is built almost entirely on *practice* — chairs, timing,
chemistry, the book, the rent. It has almost nothing on the **salon as a
political institution**, which is precisely the ground the barbershop pack does
occupy via the argument boards. The scholarship exists and it is strong.

1. **Tiffany M. Gill, *Beauty Shop Politics: African American Women's Activism
   in the Beauty Industry*, University of Illinois Press, 2010.** The direct
   counterpart to Mills, and the single biggest gap. Gill's argument: Black
   beauticians in the Jim Crow era converted **economic independence** — they
   answered to a Black clientele, so white employers had no leverage over them —
   plus **access to a public community space** into a platform for activism.
   [Publisher](https://www.press.uillinois.edu/books/?id=p076961) ·
   [UT Austin Q&A with Gill](https://liberalarts.utexas.edu/history/news/feature-archive/q-a-beauty-shop-politics.html)

2. **Bernice Robinson — the beautician who taught the first Citizenship
   School.** A **41-year-old Charleston beautician**, Robinson became the first
   teacher of the **Highlander Folk School's Citizenship Education Program** in
   the South Carolina Sea Islands. Women prepared for **voter-registration
   literacy tests in her shop**, at night, in secret. Highlander founder Myles
   Horton's stated reasoning is the whole argument in one sentence: *"we needed
   to build around black people who could stand up against white opposition, so
   black beauticians were very important."*
   This is authorable and the pack has nothing like it. It is the salon's
   equivalent of the barbershop's organising history, and it is arguably
   sharper, because the economic independence is the *mechanism*, not the
   backdrop.
   [Delaware Public Media on Gill's work](https://www.delawarepublic.org/culture-lifestyle-sports/2017-03-12/beauty-shop-politics-the-intersection-of-civil-rights-era-feminism-activism) ·
   [UT Austin, "Historian Dr. Tiffany Gill sees beauty shops as birthplace of activism"](https://liberalarts.utexas.edu/caaas/news/historian-dr-tiffany-gill-sees-beauty-shops-as-birthplace-of-activism)

3. **Annie Turnbo Malone and Poro College.** Named in *Hair Story* alongside
   Walker as a founding hair-care entrepreneur. Neither woman appears anywhere
   in the salon pack, which currently has no historical depth field at all — its
   deepest date is "1984, a rod on every section."

4. **The CROWN Act itself.** The pack has hair-as-politics boards
   (`streak`/`streak` — the silver streak refused since 2009; `spinal`/`plain`)
   but never names the live legal fact. A clue can carry it without a number:
   the law is named for what it protects, it started in California in 2019, and
   it is still not federal.

5. **Braiding licensure.** The `salon` pack has a braider (`places`/`space` —
   the back room given to a young braider for a year) but does not know that
   braiders have been a **licensing battleground**: when the Institute for
   Justice launched its braiding initiative in 2014, **24 states** required
   natural-hair braiders to hold a full barber or cosmetology licence — training
   in manicures and bleaching they would never use. A federal judge **struck down
   Utah's scheme on 8 August 2012** (Jestina Clayton), calling it "wholly
   irrational."
   [Institute for Justice, braiding](https://ij.org/issues/economic-liberty/braiding/) ·
   [NPR Planet Money on the Utah ruling](https://www.npr.org/sections/money/2012/08/09/158498244/its-now-legal-to-braid-hair-in-utah-without-a-license)
   This connects the salon pack's ownership spine to the barbershop pack's
   licensing history — the same weapon, aimed at women, still in use.

6. **The taper lever** (barbershop) — the ~1/16-inch adjustment made without
   changing guards. Concrete, visible, and the kind of specific object the
   voice note asks for.

7. **Mills' inversion** (barbershop) — that nineteenth-century Black barbers
   were required by custom to serve **only white clients**, and that the Black
   barbershop as a Black room is a post-1890 achievement. The pack treats the
   shop's Blackness as timeless. It was won.
