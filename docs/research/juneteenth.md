# juneteenth — grounding research

21 boards, 126 clues, `theme: "juneteenth"` in `data/themes.json`.

Scope note: a large share of this pack is **invented local colour** — the pit
crew lighting the fire Thursday, the chairwoman's paper call list, the teenager
assigned to the ninety-year-old, the lard in the teacake dough. These are not
false, they are unsourceable by construction: they describe a composite Texas
Juneteenth that no single documented observance is. They are handled as a class
at the bottom of this file rather than one at a time. What follows first is
every clue that makes a **checkable claim about a date, a number, a name, a
place or a text.**

## VERIFIED

### General Order No. 3 — date, issuer, text

- **base `thread` / `date`** — "June nineteenth, not the first of January. Texas counts from when the word actually landed."
- **base `herald` / `heard`** — "Galveston, June nineteenth, and Texas finally got the news it had been kept from."
- **base `dreamt` / `read`** — "What the man at the podium does slowly while everyone stands: no commentary, start to finish."

Maj. Gen. Gordon Granger, Galveston, 19 June 1865. The original handwritten
order book entry is held by the National Archives, which publishes the full text.
Source: [National Archives, "National Archives Safeguards Original 'Juneteenth' General Order"](https://www.archives.gov/news/articles/juneteenth-original-document) *(primary)*.

### The gap from the Emancipation Proclamation

- **base `dearly` / `delay`** — "Thirty months of it, with the paper already law the whole time. That is what the date is about."
- **base `herald` / `hard`** — "What thirty months of being kept in the dark was…"
- **base `depart` / `depart`** — "…two and a half years late, on foot."

1 January 1863 → 19 June 1865 is two years and roughly five and a half months.
"Two and a half years" is the standard formulation and is what Opal Lee's 2.5-mile
walk encodes; "thirty months" is the same rounding stated in months and is fine.
Source: [NMAAHC, "Who Is Opal Lee?"](https://nmaahc.si.edu/explore/stories/who-opal-lee) *(scholarship)*.

### Two thousand troops

- **base `dreams` / `arms`** — "What two thousand Union soldiers carried into Galveston, and the reason the paper got obeyed."

"More than two thousand Federal soldiers of the 13th Army Corps" arrived with
Granger; the 29th United States Colored Troops was also in the city.
Source: [Galveston Historical Foundation, "Juneteenth and General Orders, No. 3"](https://www.galvestonhistory.org/news/juneteenth-and-general-order-no-3) *(scholarship)*.

### "Absolute equality of personal rights" — five words

- **base `flared` / `real`** — "Absolute equality of personal rights: five words in the order…"

Exact and correctly counted. The order reads "This involves an absolute equality
of personal rights and rights of property between former masters and slaves."
Source: National Archives, above *(primary)*.

### Employer and hired labour / work for wages

- **base `dreamt` / `term`** — "Employer and hired labor: the words the order swapped in…"
- **base `harden` / `earn`** — "What the order said the freed would now do for wages, on the same ground, for the same man."
- **base `parked` / `reap`** — "What the order never mentioned: land…"

All three are exact. The order says the relationship "becomes that between
employer and hired labor," advises the freedmen "to remain quietly at their
present homes and work for wages," and says nothing whatever about land.
Source: National Archives, above *(primary)*.

### Galveston, not Washington

- **base `beards` / `based`** — "Galveston. Not Washington, not Richmond…"

Correct as to where the order was issued and read.
Source: Galveston Historical Foundation, above *(scholarship)*.

### Emancipation Park — 1872, ten acres, $800, Yates

- **base `parked` / `parked`** — "Ten acres in Houston, 1872, bought by freedmen because every other green space was closed to them."
- **base `thread` / `earth`** — "Ten acres of it in Houston, bought in 1872 by four men who had been born owned."

**Ten acres and 1872 are both correct**, and the price was **$800** — the two
details the authoring agent most suspected both hold. See WRONG below for the
"four men" framing, which is the part that does not.
Sources: [Historical Marker Database, "The Legacy of Emancipation Park"](https://www.hmdb.org/m.asp?m=171255) *(primary — marker text)*;
[City of Houston Archaeological & Historical Commission landmark designation, Emancipation Park](https://www.houstontx.gov/planning/HistoricPres/landmarks/07PL46_Emancipation_Park_3018_Dowling_Street.pdf) *(primary)*.

### The city took title in 1916

- **base `parked` / `park`** — "What Houston took title to in 1916…"

Correct; the park passed to the City of Houston in 1916 as a donation. The
sharper related fact, not currently used: from 1922 it was for years the only
public park in Houston open to Black residents.
Source: City of Houston landmark designation, above *(primary)*.

### The Juneteenth flag — Ben Haith, 1997

- **base `charts` / `star`** — "The Texas one, bursting on the flag Ben Haith drew in 1997, over an arc for the new horizon."

Haith (National Juneteenth Celebration Foundation) designed it in 1997; the
illustration was finalised by Lisa Jeanne Graf. The bursting star is a nova — a
new beginning; the star reads both as the Lone Star and as freedom in all fifty
states; the arc is a new horizon; red/white/blue asserts American citizenship.
The clue's compression is accurate.
Source: [CNN, "Juneteenth flag meaning"](https://www.cnn.com/2022/06/17/us/juneteenth-flag-meaning-explainer-cec) *(journalism of record)*.

### Federal holiday, 2021

- **base `fedora` / `fed`** — "What it became in 2021…"

Signed 17 June 2021 as Public Law 117-17, the Juneteenth National Independence
Day Act.
Source: [Public Law 117-17 (GovInfo)](https://www.govinfo.gov/app/details/PLAW-117publ17) *(primary)*.

### Opal Lee's walk, at eighty-nine

- **base `admire` / `idea`** — "Whose it was to walk from Fort Worth toward Washington at eighty-nine, and keep walking until it passed."

She was 89 (b. Oct 1926) when she set out from Fort Worth in September 2016,
walking 2.5 miles a day for the two and a half years, reaching Washington in
January 2017 with roughly 1.5 million signatures. **She has always been explicit
that she did not walk the full 1,400 miles** — it was a relay of symbolic
segments through Shreveport, Little Rock, St. Louis, Chicago, Atlanta and
others. The clue's "toward Washington" and "keep walking until it passed"
survive that; a clue that said she *walked from Fort Worth to Washington* would
not, so do not tighten it in that direction.
Sources: [Britannica, "Opal Lee"](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Opal-Lee) *(scholarship)*;
[Houston Public Media (Sept 2016), "This 89-Year-Old Is Walking To Washington"](https://www.houstonpublicmedia.org/articles/news/2016/09/06/166981/this-89-year-old-is-walking-to-washington/) *(contemporaneous journalism)*.

### Big Red

- **base `soared` / `soda`** — "Big Red, by the case, and in this state that is not a trend or a joke."

Created 1937 in Waco, Texas as Sun Tang Red Cream Soda; renamed Big Red in 1969.
Its standing at Texas barbecue and Juneteenth tables is well documented.
Sources: [Texas Monthly, "Texas Primer: Big Red"](https://www.texasmonthly.com/being-texan/texas-primer-big-red/) *(journalism of record)*;
[Texas Highways, "Big Red Is Texas Barbecue's Unofficial Signature Soda"](https://texashighways.com/food-drink/big-red-is-texas-barbecues-unofficial-signature-soda/) *(journalism of record)*.

### Freedom colonies

- **base `traced` / `acre`** — "Freedom colonies: bought cheap, worked hard, and a few of those towns are still on the map."

An estimated 557 freedom colonies were founded in Texas 1865–1930, land acquired
by cash purchase or adverse possession, often in flood-prone bottomland. This is
the best-documented "Texas specificity" claim in the pack.
Sources: [The Texas Freedom Colonies Project (Andrea Roberts)](https://andrearobertsphd.com/the-texas-freedom-colonies-project/) *(scholarship)*;
[TSHA Handbook, "Freedmen's Settlements"](https://www.tshaonline.org/handbook/entries/freedmens-settlements) *(scholarship)*.

### Freedmen's Town brick streets

- **base `thread` / `tread`** — "What the brick streets of Freedmen's Town were laid for, by hand, by the people living on them."

Residents paved their own streets with hand-made brick after the city declined
to; the district is described as the largest intact freed-slave settlement
remaining in the United States. Note the "by hand" element rests substantially
on oral tradition (Rev. Jeremiah Smith's congregation and Andrews Street), which
is the appropriate tier of source for it, not a weakness.
Sources: [BlackPast, "Freedmen's Town, Houston, Texas (1865– )"](https://blackpast.org/african-american-history/freedmens-town-houston-texas-1865/) *(scholarship)*;
[ABC13 Houston, "Bricks of Freedmen's Town"](https://abc13.com/freedmens-town-bricks-fourth-ward-andrews-street/13385784/) *(community/journalism)*.

### The Strand

- **base `grated` / `great`** — "Her grandmother's grandmother stood on the Strand in 1865."

Defensible and better than the balcony version: the reading at the Osterman
Building was at the corner of Strand and 22nd.
Source: Galveston Historical Foundation, above *(scholarship)*.

## WRONG

### 1. The order is FOUR sentences, not five — used twice

- **base `shared` / `reads`** — "How the order goes: the plainest language the government ever managed, and only five sentences."
- **base `beards` / `bared`** — "What the order did in five sentences, about a thing every person in the room already knew."

The National Archives text is four sentences: (1) all slaves are free; (2)
absolute equality / employer and hired labor; (3) freedmen advised to remain and
work for wages; (4) not allowed to collect at military posts or be supported in
idleness. Some renderings collapse it to three. Nobody counts five. This is the
single most-repeated factual error in the pack and it is in the pack's most
quotable clue.
Source: [National Archives](https://www.archives.gov/news/articles/juneteenth-original-document) *(primary)*.

Drop-in replacements (both 30–120 chars, neither contains the answer or a
four-character stem of it):

- `reads` → `How the order goes: four sentences, the plainest language the government ever managed.` (86)
- `bared` → `What four sentences did, about a thing every person standing there already knew.` (79)

### 2. Emancipation Park was not bought by four men

- **base `thread` / `earth`** — "Ten acres of it in Houston, bought in 1872 by four men who had been born owned."

The "four men" framing (Allen, Brock, Yates, Dibble) is the popular short form
and describes the *leadership*, not the purchasers. The historical marker names
**nine trustees** of the Colored People of Harris County Festival Association —
Richard Brock, Richard Allen, Daniel Riley, John Graham, Taylor Burke, Frank
Keeland, Johnson Rice, John Sessums and Tillman Bush — buying on behalf of a
subscribing community. The pack's own `parked` clue already gets this right
("bought by freedmen"), so the two clues currently contradict each other.
Source: [Historical Marker Database, "The Legacy of Emancipation Park"](https://www.hmdb.org/m.asp?m=171255) *(primary)*.

- `earth` → `Ten acres of it in Houston, 1872, bought by nine trustees for eight hundred dollars.` (83)

### 3. 1866 was the FIRST observance, not the second

- **base `parked` / `dare`** — "What it took to hold the second one in 1866, in a state that had just lost and was not sorry."

19 June 1865 was the day of the order, not a celebration of it. The first
recorded public observance of the anniversary was in **1866**, in Houston — a
parade led by Elias Dibble as president and Sandy Parker as marshal, to a grove
for speeches, music and a meal. So 1866 is the first one, and the clue is off by
one in a way a Texas reader will catch immediately.
Sources: [Britannica, "Juneteenth"](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Juneteenth) *(scholarship)*;
[Rice University historian on the 1866 Houston observance (KXAN)](https://www.kxan.com/news/texas/first-juneteenth-celebration-was-held-in-houston-rice-scholar-says/) *(journalism)*.

- `dare` → `What it took to hold the very first one in 1866, in a state that had lost and was not sorry.` (91)

### 4. "Restore" is the wrong verb for Emancipation Avenue

- **base `braved` / `ave`** — "Emancipation, in Houston: the street the parade uses, and the name the neighborhood fought to restore."

Houston City Council voted unanimously in January 2017 to rename **Dowling
Street** — named for Confederate commander Richard "Dick" Dowling — with the
change timed to Juneteenth 2017 and the $33m park reopening. The street never
carried the name before, so nothing was *restored*; it was won. The push was led
by Rep. Garnet Coleman.
Sources: [City of Houston press release, "Dowling Street Renamed to Emancipation Avenue"](https://www.houstontx.gov/planning/AboutPD/news_releases17/Renaming-of-Dowling_Press-Release.pdf) *(primary)*;
[Houston Public Media (Jan 2017)](https://www.houstonpublicmedia.org/articles/news/local/2017/01/12/183416/houstons-dowling-street-to-be-renamed-emancipation-avenue/) *(journalism)*.

- `ave` → `Emancipation, in Houston: the street the parade uses, and a Confederate's name taken off it in 2017.` (99)

### 5. The Emancipation Proclamation years

- **base `hearty` / `year`** — "Eighteen sixty-three, sixty-four, sixty-five — all of them after the paper was already signed."

1863 is not "after the paper was already signed" — the Proclamation took effect
1 January 1863, so the first of the three years is the year of signing, not a
year after it. Small, but it is arithmetic on the pack's central fact.
Source: National Archives, above *(primary)*.

- `year` → `Sixty-three, sixty-four, sixty-five: the paper took effect at the start of the first one.` (88)

## UNVERIFIABLE

### The Ashton Villa balcony — the pack asserts it twice

- **base `herald` / `herald`** — "In 1865 the word still meant a man on a balcony with somebody else's paper in his hand."
- **base `fedora` / `era`** — "What ended at Ashton Villa, and what began the next morning, which was the harder part."

The authoring agent was right to flag this. **There is no contemporaneous
evidence that Granger read the order from the balcony of Ashton Villa.** The
documented sites are the Union headquarters at the Osterman Building (Strand and
22nd), the 1861 Custom House, the courthouse, and the "Negro Church," today's
Reedy Chapel-AME on Broadway. The balcony is local folklore, reinforced annually
by a Sons of Union Veterans reenactor performing the reading *at* Ashton Villa —
a commemoration that has been widely mistaken for the event it commemorates.

`herald` is defensible as written, because it describes what the *word* herald
meant, not what happened at Galveston, and a balcony is generic to it. `era` is
not: it names Ashton Villa as the place a thing ended, which is precisely the
claim that fails. Recommend correcting `era` and leaving `herald` to a human on
tone grounds.

- `era` → `What ended on the Strand in 1865, and what began the next morning, which was the harder part.` (92)

Sources: [Galveston Historical Foundation](https://www.galvestonhistory.org/news/juneteenth-and-general-order-no-3) *(scholarship)*;
[Texas A&M Galveston, "Juneteenth Holiday Holds Special Meaning in Galveston"](https://news.galveston.tamu.edu/juneteenth-holiday-holds-special-meaning-in-galveston/) *(scholarship)*.

### The red drink's West African lineage — asserted more confidently everywhere than the evidence supports

- **base `framed` / `red`** — "Hibiscus once, strawberry soda now, and the color was never the point of it."

This is the claim the authoring agent asked about specifically, and the answer
is: **it is an inference by named food scholars, repeated until it reads as
settled fact, and there is no documentary chain.** What actually exists:

- Frederick Douglass Opie says the tradition "may come from" hibiscus tea drunk
  at celebrations, and reports that "some historians believe" the red links to
  Asante and Yoruba blood offerings — hedged language in both halves.
- Adrian Miller traces red drink to two native West African plants, kola and
  hibiscus (bissap).
- Michael Twitty says the practice "may owe its existence to" enslaved Yoruba
  and Kongo people brought to Texas.

Three respected scholars, three modal verbs. Nobody produces a Texas Juneteenth
red-drink record continuous with a West African one; the argument is from
plant provenance, colour symbolism and plausibility. That is a real scholarly
position and worth repeating — but the pack should not out-assert its sources.

The clue as written is actually the safest formulation in wide circulation: it
says hibiscus *once* and strawberry soda *now* without claiming an unbroken
line, and "the color was never the point" sidesteps the causal claim entirely.
**Recommend keeping it and refusing any edit that hardens it into lineage.**
Sources: [Smithsonian Magazine, "A Brief History of Red Drink"](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/arts-culture/a-brief-history-of-red-drink-180980046/) *(journalism of record; would not serve content to this fetch — cited from search summary, verify before canon)*;
[Atlas Obscura, "The Story Behind Red-Hued Juneteenth Food and Drink"](https://www.atlasobscura.com/articles/what-is-juneteenth) *(journalism)*.

### Texas schools letting out for the day

- **base `grated` / `grade`** — "What Texas schools let out for long before any calendar up north carried the day at all."

Could not source, and the statutory position argues against it. Emancipation Day
became a Texas state holiday under Al Edwards' HB 1016 (66th Legislature, 1979;
Acts 1979 ch. 481), effective 1980 — but it is a **partial-staffing** holiday
under Gov't Code § 662.003, not a full closure, and it governs *state employees*,
not school districts. Texas school years also end before 19 June in most
districts, so there is generally nothing to let out from. Searched: TEA calendar
rules, § 662.003, district calendar coverage, HB 1016 text. Found nothing
establishing a schools-closed tradition.

Recommend replacing with the sourceable version of the same idea — Texas was
first, by four decades:

- `grade` → `What Texas gave the day in 1980, first in the nation, when no calendar up north carried it.` (89)

Sources: [Texas Legislative Reference Library, HB 1016, 66th R.S.](https://lrl.texas.gov/legis/billSearch/BillDetails.cfm?legSession=66-0&billTypeDetail=HB&billnumberdetail=1016) *(primary)*;
[Tex. Gov't Code § 662.003](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/SOTWDocs/GV/htm/GV.662.htm) *(primary)*.

### The segregated fairgrounds gate

- **base `grated` / `gate`** — "The one at the county fairgrounds that stayed shut to them, which is why the rodeo has its own."

The *general* claim is documented: Black cowboys were excluded from the
mainstream rodeo circuit and Black communities across East Texas ran their own
rodeos for generations, many tied to Juneteenth; Sarah Bird photographed them
in the late 1970s and UT Press published the work as *Juneteenth Rodeo* (2023).
What could not be sourced is the specific causal mechanism the clue asserts — a
county fairgrounds gate shut to Black entrants as the origin of a separate
rodeo. That is a reasonable synthesis, not a documented one. Low risk; flagged
for completeness.
Sources: [Texas Highways, "Black Rodeos Are a Storied Texas Tradition"](https://texashighways.com/culture/black-rodeos-are-a-storied-texas-tradition/) *(journalism)*;
[UT Press, *Juneteenth Rodeo* (Sarah Bird)](https://utpress.utexas.edu/9781477329542/) *(scholarship)*;
[Bullock Texas State History Museum, "Juneteenth Rodeo"](https://www.thestoryoftexas.com/resources/juneteenth-rodeo/) *(primary — museum)*.

### The pageant

- **base `herald` / `held`**, **`hearty` / `rate`**, **`beards` / `beads`**, **`flared` / `flared`**, **`soared` / `rode`**, **`admire` / `ride`**

Miss Juneteenth and Little Miss Juneteenth pageants are documented as a
widespread tradition in Galveston and across Black communities, judged on essay,
interview, talent and evening gown. So the *tradition* is real and the pack is
right to build on it. The pack's specific scoring claim — **`rate`**: "What the
judges score first: the recitation. Poise second. The dress not at all." —
contradicts the documented rubric, which does include evening gown. Recommend
softening.

- `rate` → `What the judges do with the essay and the interview before anybody walks in a gown.` (81)

Sources: [Oxford American, "Miss Juneteenth"](https://oxfordamerican.org/on-jubilee-a-juneteenth-series/miss-juneteenth) *(community/essay)*;
[Mic, "The importance of the legendary Miss Juneteenth beauty pageant"](https://www.mic.com/life/miss-juneteenth-pageant-legacy) *(journalism)*.

### How the day spread beyond Texas

- **base `dearly` / `relay`** — "How the word actually traveled in 1865: port to the next place, and the next, by mouth."
- **base `harden` / `near`** — "Sixty miles between the port and places nobody told, and the road was open the whole time."
- **base `derail` / `lied`** — "What the planters told the people through two cotton seasons, until one of them finally slipped."

The general history — Juneteenth spreading with Black Texans' migration, first
across Texas and the Gulf, later nationally — is documented at survey level.
The pack's specific mechanisms are not. In particular **`lied`** asserts a
coordinated planter conspiracy of concealment as fact. The scholarship treats
delayed news in Texas as a mix of minimal Union presence, deliberate withholding
by individual enslavers, and distance — with no single agreed explanation, and
the "the news was deliberately kept for two more cotton harvests" version
circulating as folk explanation more than established finding. **`near`**'s
"sixty miles" is an invented figure with no source.

Searched: TSHA, Britannica, Galveston Historical Foundation, History.com's
"Why It Took 2.5 Years" piece. None supports a specific distance or a
documented conspiracy. Recommend `near` lose the number:

- `near` → `The distance between the port and places nobody told, with the road open the whole time.` (86)

`lied` is the higher-risk one and should go to the human reader rather than be
silently rewritten — it is a claim about who did what to whom.
Source: [History.com, "Juneteenth: Why It Took 2.5 Years for Freedom to Reach Texas"](https://www.history.com/articles/juneteenth-emancipation-proclamation-texas) *(journalism)*.

## TONE — NEEDS A HUMAN

1. **`herald`/`herald` — the balcony.** The factual finding is above: undocumented.
   The question research cannot settle is whether writing the balcony into the
   pack's opening board reads as honouring the folklore a community has kept, or
   as a national product repeating a myth back to the people who know better.
   Galveston stages the reenactment itself, which is an argument on both sides.

2. **`derail`/`lied` — "What the planters told the people."** Whether to state
   deliberate concealment as flat fact in a word game. Defensible; also the
   pack's strongest accusation, made in twelve words, unsourced.

3. **`hearty`/`yeah` — "What comes back from the crowd at the line about all
   slaves being free. Not applause. Just that."** The single best-written clue
   in the pack and the one most dependent on the author having been in the room.

4. **`admire`/`media` — "The trucks that turn up in 2021 and ask an elder to
   explain her whole life in thirty seconds."** A commercial product making that
   joke is inside the joke. Needs a reader who can say whether it lands.

5. **Whether the pack is too Houston/Galveston.** Every geographic anchor is
   Emancipation Park, the Strand, Freedmen's Town, Emancipation Avenue. Accurate
   as origin, but Juneteenth is now national, and a reader from a place that has
   kept it for eighty years without a Gulf coast may or may not find themselves
   in this.
