20 boards, 120 clues. Checked against published sources per `docs/research/README.md`.
Clues are quoted exactly as they appear in `data/themes.json`, with base and slot.

## VERIFIED

### The premise: reunion as post-Emancipation institution reinforced by the Migration

The whole pack rests on this and it holds. Two distinct origins, both documented:

1. **Post-Emancipation family reconstitution.** Freedpeople searched for kin sold
   away, using newspaper advertisements, letters and word of mouth; the
   "Information Wanted" ad corpus runs to thousands of items. The Smithsonian's
   NMAAHC treats the modern reunion as a direct descendant of that search.
   - <https://nmaahc.si.edu/explore/stories/historical-legacy-black-family-reunions>
   - Primary-source corpus of the ads: *Last Seen: Finding Family After Slavery*,
     Villanova / Mother Bethel AME — <https://informationwanted.org/>
2. **The Great Migration made the reunion annual and interstate.** ~6 million
   people left the South 1910–1970 (Census). Once branches were 700 miles apart,
   the summer trip back became the mechanism that held the family together.
   - Census Bureau, *The Great Migration, 1910 to 1970*:
     <https://www.census.gov/dataviz/visualizations/020>
   - National Archives, Migrations and the Black Experience:
     <https://www.archives.gov/research/african-americans/migrations>

So the pack's core framing — a reunion is a Migration artifact, not a generic
family picnic — is sourced, not a vibe.

### The three streams the pack uses as structure — correct as drawn

Wilkerson's three streams (*The Warmth of Other Suns*, Random House 2010,
pp. 10–11 of the prologue as usually cited):

| Stream | Origin | Destination | Pack board |
|---|---|---|---|
| East coast | FL, GA, the Carolinas, VA | DC, Philadelphia, New York, Boston | `badges`, `bodega`, `branch`/`car` |
| Central spine | MS, AL, TN, AR | Chicago, Detroit, Cleveland, Milwaukee, Pittsburgh | `branch`, `vendor`/`drove`, `anemic` |
| West | LA, TX | California | *not used in this pack — see MATERIAL* |

Statistical backing for the two the pack leans on hardest:

- **Mississippi → Chicago.** Chicago-born-in-Mississippi residents rose from
  4,612 (1910) to 19,485 (1920); Chicago's Black population went 44,103 (1910) →
  233,903 (1930), ~94% of that increase from Southern migration. The Illinois
  Central ran New Orleans–Jackson–Memphis–Chicago; Pullman porters carried the
  *Chicago Defender* south, which ran the "Great Northern Drive" of May 15, 1917.
  - WTTW, *Early Chicago: The Great Migration* —
    <https://www.wttw.com/dusable-to-obama/the-great-migration>
  - Mississippi Blues Trail, "Mississippi to Chicago" —
    <https://msbluestrail.org/blues-trail-markers/mississippi-to-chicago>
- **Alabama → Detroit.** Detroit's Black population: 5,471 (1910) → 40,838 (1920),
  a 611% increase, doubling again by 1930. Alabama, Georgia, Tennessee, South
  Carolina and Mississippi supplied a high share. Ford employed roughly half of
  the ~25,000 Black autoworkers in the US by 1929.
  - Encyclopedia of Alabama, "Great Migration from Alabama" —
    <https://encyclopediaofalabama.org/article/great-migration-from-alabama/>
  - Bentley Historical Library, *Goin' North: Black Detroit and the Great
    Migration, 1910–1930* — <https://lsa.umich.edu/daas/news-events/all-events.detail.html/39296-7918413.html>

**`branch` / `branch`** — "The Chicago side has it this year, and the program
lists all six in the order they went north." VERIFIED as a practice-shaped claim:
listing branches by migration order is exactly the ordering logic a reunion
program uses, and the Chicago side of a Mississippi family is the single most
statistically typical Northern branch in the country.

**`vendor` / `drove`** — "Eleven hours up from Detroit with three cousins and one

Sound.

**`anemic` / `cinema`** — "The old one on Woodward." Woodward Avenue is Detroit's

**`badges` / `gas`** — "Two hundred and forty dollars of it up I-75 and back."
I-75 *is* the Detroit ↔ South corridor (Detroit–Toledo–Cincinnati–Chattanooga–
Atlanta). $240 round-trip is right for ~1,500 mi in a van. See WRONG below for
the board it is sitting in.

### The reunion apparatus is documented practice, not one family's habit

This was the author's biggest exposure and it survives. Dr. Ione D. Vargus
founded the **Family Reunion Institute at Temple University in 1990** (research
from 1986) and ran a national family-reunion conference for 17 years; her own
framing is that Black family reunions have become "structured and somewhat
institutionalized." That is the citation that converts the pack's committee /
treasurer / program / historian furniture from anecdote into documented form.

- Temple University, "Family reunions create lasting bonds" —
  <https://news.temple.edu/news/family-reunions-create-lasting-bonds>
- Family Reunion Institute — <https://nationalfamilyreunioninstitute.net/faq>
- Deseret News on the Institute's research —
  <https://www.deseret.com/2021/5/19/22440615/>
- Chicago Crusader on Vargus —
  <https://chicagocrusader.com/dr-ione-d-vargus-family-reunions-matter/>

Specifically corroborated elements: the **souvenir program/booklet**, the
**group photograph**, the **T-shirt bearing surname, family tree or motto**, the
**planning committee with officers**, and the **elder-testimony / legacy segment**.
NMAAHC and the AFRO both describe the commemorative program + photo + T-shirt
triad as standard.

- AFRO, "Family reunions: A tradition rooted in Black history" —
  <https://afro.com/black-family-reunions-preserving-history/>

This covers, as documented practice: `family`/`family` (tree on the shirt),
`family`/`aim` (stated purpose on page one), `budget`/`budget` (printed budget),
`donate`/`donate` + `donate`/`ante` + `counts`/`cost` (per-head dues),
`hotels`/`hotels` (room block), `albums`/`albums`, `magnet`/`magnet` (souvenir

`golden`/`gold` (host-branch shirt color), `legacy`/`legacy` + `donate`/`neat`
(the historian's binder).

### `blocks` — the Philadelphia street-closure detail is exact

**`blocks` / `blocks`** — "The city permit closes two of them off, corner to
corner, noon until eight on Saturday." Philadelphia block-party permits close a
street corner-to-corner and **the street must reopen no later than 8:30 p.m.**
Weekend permit fee is $25 if filed 21+ days ahead ($60 late); application must be
in at least six weeks prior with a petition signed by 75% of households on the
block.

- City of Philadelphia, "Apply to hold a block party or street event" —
  <https://www.phila.gov/services/streets-sidewalks-alleys/street-closures/apply-to-hold-a-block-party-or-street-event/>
- Phila. Code § 11-105 —
  <https://codelibrary.amlegal.com/codes/philadelphia/latest/philadelphia_pa/0-0-0-284044>

"noon until eight" is not a guess; it is the ordinance. Best-sourced clue in the
pack.

### The land boards — the history under them is real and enormous

**`graves` / `gave`** — "What the great-uncle did with forty acres in 1948, and
why nobody argues about it now."
**`marked` / `dream`** — "What the great-uncle called the forty acres, and the
deed is in her binder."
**`matron` / `manor`** — "What the deed calls the home place, though it is four
rooms and a tin roof."

The dispossession these sit on top of:

- Black farmers held **~16–16.6 million acres in 1910** (925,708 Black farm
  operators at the 1920 peak); **4.7 million acres by 2017**, ~0.5% of US
  farmland — a ~90% loss.
- Estimated value of the 20th-century loss: **$326 billion** (Francis et al.,
  *AEA Papers and Proceedings*, 2022).
- **Heirs' property is the specific mechanism.** Land passed without a will
  becomes a tenancy-in-common among dozens of heirs; any single fractional owner
  — or a speculator who buys one share — can force a **partition sale**, a
  court-ordered auction typically below market. USDA calls heirs' property
  **"the leading cause of Black involuntary land loss."** Heirs' property is
  ~3.5 million acres across the South, >1/3 of Southern Black-owned land,
  >$28 billion in value.
- The remedy is the **Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act** (Uniform Law
  Commission, 2010; first enactments 2011), now in 22 states + DC: right of
  first refusal for co-tenants, appraisal at fair market value, open-market sale
  before auction.

Sources:
- USDA FSA, "What You Need to Know About Heirs' Property" —
  <https://www.fsa.usda.gov/sites/default/files/documents/whatuneedtoknow_heirprop.pdf>
- Union of Concerned Scientists, *Lost Inheritance* —
  <https://www.ucs.org/resources/lost-inheritance>
- Grist, "What is heirs' property?" —
  <https://grist.org/fix/justice/what-is-heirs-property-a-huge-contributor-to-black-land-loss-you-might-not-have-heard-of/>
- Uniform Law Commission, UPHPA —
  <https://www.uniformlaws.org/committees/community-home?CommunityKey=50724584-e808-4255-bc5d-8ea4e588371d>
- Francis, Hamilton, Black et al., "Black Land Loss: 1920–1997," *AEA P&P* 112
  (2022) — <https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/pandp.20221015>

**Verdict on tone:** `gave` and `dream` are the two clues in the pack that need
the most care, and as written they pass. `gave` frames the acres as a gift that
*settled* an argument — the opposite of flippant, and it is the counter-case to
partition, since a documented 1948 conveyance is precisely what prevents an
heirs'-property fight. `dream` puts the deed **in the binder** — i.e. the family
did the paperwork. Both clues are on the side of the documented remedy rather
than making a joke of the loss. Do not soften them; do not add a punchline.

### `bodega` / `ago` and `badges` / `based` — Philadelphia union halls and North Philly

**`bodega` / `ago`** — "Fifty years since the first one, in a union hall on Ridge

### `legacy` / `clay` — red clay

"What the cemetery ground is: red, heavy." Piedmont and much of the Deep South

GA/AL/SC/NC/MS-hill-country burying grounds. (Not accurate for the Delta, which
is alluvial black gumbo — but no clue places the cemetery in the Delta.)

## WRONG

### `branch` / `arch` — St. Louis is defensible, but the clue's history is off

> **`branch` / `arch`:** "What the drive up stops under in St. Louis, halfway
> along the road their grandmother took."

Two separate questions, and they land differently.

**Is St. Louis on a migration route? Yes — genuinely.** St. Louis was a major
Great Migration destination and staging city in its own right, drawing from
Arkansas, the Missouri Bootheel, Tennessee and Mississippi; East St. Louis was
significant enough as a migrant-labor destination to produce the 1917 East St.
Louis massacre. So the geography is not invented.

**Is it on *this family's* road? Partly.** The board is explicitly a Chicago
branch of a Mississippi family. The rail route those migrants took was the
**Illinois Central**: Jackson → Memphis → Cairo → Champaign → Chicago. That line
does **not** pass through St. Louis. The modern *driving* route does — I-55 runs
Memphis → St. Louis → Chicago — so a family driving up today does stop under the
Arch. The clue says the drive stops there, which is right.

**What is actually wrong is the timing.** The Gateway Arch was **topped out
October 28, 1965**, opened to the public **June 10, 1967**, dedicated **May 25,
1968**. If the grandmother made the trip during the Migration proper, there was
no Arch to pass under. "halfway along the road their grandmother took" reads as
if she saw it. She didn't.

- History.com, "St. Louis' Gateway Arch is completed" —
  <https://www.history.com/this-day-in-history/october-28/gateway-arch-completed>
- NPS, Gateway Arch National Park history —
  <https://www.nps.gov/jeff/learn/historyculture/index.htm>

Drop-in replacement (74 chars, no answer or first four chars in text):

> `arch`: "The drive north stops under it in St. Louis, and she was never there to see it."

Or, if you want to keep the waypoint and lose the ancestry claim (91 chars):

> `arch`: "St. Louis, hour six, and the cousins get out to take the same photograph every year."

Second option is the safer one — it keeps the migration-corridor feel and makes
no historical claim at all.

### `badges` / `gas` — I-75 is in the wrong board

> **`badges` / `based`:** "The committee is run out of one apartment in North
> Philadelphia, and has been for nine years."
> **`badges` / `gas`:** "Two hundred and forty dollars of it up I-75 and back,
> split four ways at the first stop."

Both are fine clues. They are not fine in the same board. **I-75 does not go
near Philadelphia** — it runs Sault Ste. Marie–Detroit–Toledo–Cincinnati–

I-95 or the Turnpike; I-75 belongs to the Detroit boards (`vendor`, `anemic`),

these as one family, and this family cannot have both.

Drop-in replacement for the Philadelphia board (79 chars):

> `gas`: "Two hundred and forty dollars of it down I-95 and back, split four ways at the first stop."

That is 90 chars — inside the contract. Alternatively move `gas` as written into
`anemic` or `vendor` and give `badges` a non-highway clue.

## TONE — NEEDS A HUMAN

The author flagged three. Research moves two of them and cannot settle any.

### 1. Does `bodega` read as Philadelphia or as imported New York?

**Research says: it is real in Philadelphia, but it is not neutral there.**
Philadelphia has the second-largest Puerto Rican population of any US city, and
"bodega" is in ordinary use in North Philly and Kensington. But the default
Philadelphia word for the same store, across the city and specifically in Black
Philadelphia, is **"corner store"** (and, for the Chinese-owned takeout-window
variety, "Chinese store"). "Bodega" carries a *Latino-owned* connotation in
Philly that it does not carry in New York, where it is the generic.

- Philadelphia Inquirer on Latino groceries in Philly —
  <https://www.inquirer.com/philly-tips/latino-groceries-philadelphia-20210922.html>
- Latino Rebels, "Puerto Ricans Struggle for Their Place in Philadelphia" —
  <https://www.latinorebels.com/2020/11/30/puertoricansphiladelphia/>

So: the word is not wrong, and a Black family reunion in North Philly buying ice
from a Puerto Rican-owned bodega is a true Philadelphia scene. But the base word
`bodega` is doing double duty as the board's *name*, which reads as the author
asserting it is the Philadelphia word. **A Philadelphia reader is the only one
who can say whether that lands as local or as a New Yorker writing Philly.** My
read, offered as a read and not a finding: it is closer to imported than the
author hopes, and `corner store` is what the clue text itself already says
("The corner store has held forty sacks of ice"). Consider whether the board
should be based elsewhere. **Open.**

### 2. Does naming Mississippi as the butt of a shirt-color joke land as in-family teasing?

No Mississippi clue in the current 20 boards names Mississippi as a shirt-color
butt — the shirt-color argument lives in **`golden`/`gold`** ("The shirt color
the host branch took for itself, and the argument that followed"), where the
losing party is unnamed. **If that is the clue in question, the flag is already
resolved by construction:** the joke is on the *host branch* for taking the good
color, which is teasing upward at whoever has power that year, and that is how
in-family teasing works. Naming a state as the butt is a different act, and it is
the one that would need a reader — regional teasing between branches is real and
affectionate, and it is also the exact register where an outside author sounds
like they are laughing at Mississippi rather than with the family. Research
cannot settle it. **Open**, but note it may be moot.

### 3. Is the pack over-indexed on a Southern rural home place?

**Countable, so here is the count.** Of 20 boards:

| Register | Boards | Share |
|---|---|---|
| Southern rural / home place / cemetery | `graves`, `matron`, `marked`, `legacy` | 4 (20%) |
| Urban North, explicitly placed | `branch` (Chicago/Detroit/Philly/St. Louis), `blocks`, `bodega`, `badges`, `magnet`, `anemic` (Detroit) | 6 (30%) |
| Place-neutral logistics / banquet | `family`, `hotels`, `budget`, `vendor`, `donate`, `albums`, `golden`, `counts`, `barged`, `claims` | 10 (50%) |

**The balance is now right, with one caveat.** The urban-North boards outnumber
the Southern-rural boards 6 to 4, and `blocks` does real corrective work —
"There is no home place to drive out to. There is this, and the number on the
street sign" is the single most important line in the pack, because it says out
loud that the home place is not universal. That one clue does more than the other
five urban boards combined.

The caveat: **the four rural boards are the emotionally heaviest ones.** The
cemetery, the memorial, the reading of names, the forty acres, the
ninety-one-year-old all live in the Southern-rural cluster. The urban boards are
funnier and lighter — bounce houses, dominoes, a sock in a trunk, a wedding party
barging through the photo. So the pack is numerically balanced and **tonally
still tilted**: the South is where the pack goes to be serious, the North is
where it goes to be funny. That is a real and defensible pattern (the graves *are*
in the South), but it means a Northern-born reader gets all the jokes and none of
the weight.

**Fix available without touching the ratio:** give one urban board a serious
line. `blocks` is the natural home — the block *is* the ancestral place for a
family three generations into Philadelphia, and nothing in that board says so
with any gravity. **Open for a reader, but the structural diagnosis is solid.**

### 4. Not flagged by the author, but flagging it now: `graves`/`rage`

## MATERIAL WE DON'T HAVE

Specific, sourced detail worth authoring toward.

1. **The third Wilkerson stream is missing entirely.** No board touches
   Louisiana/Texas → California. That is ~half a million people to Los Angeles
   alone by 1970, the Watts and West Oakland families, the Southern Pacific and
   the wartime shipyards (Kaiser Richmond, Hunters Point). A `reunion` pack that
   maps the Migration and omits the West Coast is telling two-thirds of the
   story. Sourced hooks: Second Great Migration 1942–1970, ~5 million people;
   California's Black population went from 124,306 (1940) to 1.4 million (1970).
   - <https://www.archives.gov/research/african-americans/migrations>
   - <https://www.inmotionaame.org/print.cfm@migration=9.html>

2. **Roots, January 1977 — the pack has a historian and a binder but no origin
   for them.** ABC aired *Roots* over eight consecutive nights, **January 23–30,
   1977**; the finale drew ~100 million viewers, ~85% of TV households. Genealogy
   inquiries at the **National Archives rose ~300%**; the Microfilm Reading Room
   in Washington had **wait lines for the first time in its history**, and
   reference mail peaked at **7,000 letters in one week**. That is the moment the
   family historian became a role, and it dates the modern reunion boom
   precisely. A `roots`-era clue would ground `legacy`, `marked`/`mark` and
   `donate`/`neat` in something specific instead of general diligence.
   - National Archives Prologue, "The 'Roots' of Genealogy at the National
     Archives" —
     <https://prologue.blogs.archives.gov/2019/02/22/the-roots-of-genealogy-at-the-national-archives>
   - AAIHS, "Black Genealogy After Alex Haley's *Roots*" —
     <https://www.aaihs.org/black-genealogy-after-alex-haleys-roots/>

3. **The 1870 census wall.** Enslaved people appear in the 1850 and 1860 censuses
   only as tick marks on slave schedules — age, sex, color, no name. **1870 is
   the first federal census to name most Black Americans**, which is why
   genealogists call it "the 1870 brick wall," and why the Freedmen's Bureau
   records and the Freedman's Savings Bank registers (which recorded parents,
   siblings and plantation of origin) matter so much. The pack's historian
   "confirms names in the county records" — the wall is the thing she is actually
   fighting, and no clue names it.
   - National Archives, African American Heritage / 1870 census —
     <https://www.archives.gov/research/african-americans/census>
   - Freedmen's Bureau records —
     <https://www.archives.gov/research/african-americans/freedmens-bureau>

4. **The National Black Family Reunion Celebration**, founded by **Dorothy
   Height** and the **National Council of Negro Women in 1986** on the National
   Mall, drawing hundreds of thousands annually and franchising to Cincinnati,
   Los Angeles, Atlanta and elsewhere. It is the reason "Black family reunion" is
   a *national* phrase and not just a family one, and it is a direct, citable
   answer to any reviewer who asks whether the pack invented an institution.
   - NCNW — <https://ncnw.org/>

5. **Reunion registries and the Black genealogy tradition as an organized
   field.** The **Afro-American Historical and Genealogical Society (AAHGS)**,
   founded **1977** in Washington DC — the same year as *Roots* — with chapters
   nationwide; and the **Black family reunion registry** tradition. AAHGS gives
   the historian cousin an actual institution to belong to.
   - AAHGS — <https://aahgs.org/>

6. **The Pigford settlements.** *Pigford v. Glickman* (1999) and *Pigford II*
   (2010) — the largest civil-rights settlements in US history, ~$1.25 billion,
   over USDA discrimination against Black farmers in loans and disaster aid. If
   the pack ever wants a clue about *why* the forty acres shrank, this is the
   documented, litigated answer rather than a vague gesture.
   - <https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/RS20430>

7. **Green Book-era travel as reunion logistics.** The pack's `car` clue
   ("Fourteen hours out of Philadelphia, two coolers on the back floor, and one
   stop the whole way") is *almost* the Green Book clue — the coolers and the
   single stop are exactly why families packed food and drove straight through.
   The *Negro Motorist Green Book* ran 1936–1967 and is fully digitized by NYPL.
   Naming that history would turn a good clue into a sourced one.
   - NYPL Digital Collections, Green Book —
     <https://digitalcollections.nypl.org/collections/the-green-book>

8. **The reunion T-shirt as a documented commercial category.** Reunion tees,
   souvenir booklets and per-branch color coding sustain an actual
   printing-and-souvenir trade; *Reunions* magazine (1990–2019) covered it as an
   industry for three decades and its archive is a practitioner-tier source for
   how these events are actually run — dues structures, committee roles, banquet
   costs. Worth a pass before the next reunion pack.
   - <https://reunionsmag.com/>
